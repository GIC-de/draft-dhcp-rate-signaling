DHCP Explicit Rate Signaling

Abstract

This document defines new Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol (DHCP) options for both DHCPv4 and DHCPv6 to signal available upstream and downstream data rates to clients and intermediate nodes. In many broadband access networks, the provisioned upstream rate is enforced at the service edge by ingress policers, while the Customer Premises Equipment (CPE) sends at the speed of its physical link without knowledge of this rate. The resulting policer drops cause unstable and reduced throughput and render upstream prioritization at the CPE ineffective. By learning the provisioned rates via DHCP, the CPE can shape its upstream traffic to these rates, moving the queue to the CPE, where it can be managed by prioritization, Active Queue Management (AQM), and the Low Latency, Low Loss, and Scalable Throughput (L4S) architecture, while enforcement at the service edge remains in place. Intermediate nodes acting as DHCP relay agents or performing DHCP snooping can use the same information to instantiate per-subscriber shapers and policers, without requiring a separate provisioning interface. The options are also applicable within local area networks, for example to inform end devices about the capacity of the upstream connection.¶

1. Introduction

1.1. Problem Statement

In broadband access networks, the rate available to a subscriber is typically lower than the speed of the physical link that connects the Customer Premises Equipment (CPE) to the network. The provisioned rate is commonly enforced at the Broadband Network Gateway (BNG) [TR101], which limits the traffic of each subscriber to the purchased service tier. In addition, the upstream rate can be limited by an access device connected to the CPE, such as an Optical Network Terminal (ONT) [G.984.1], a Digital Subscriber Line (DSL) modem, or a cable modem, or within the access network, for example by the upstream bandwidth allocation of a passive optical network, by the synchronization rate of a DSL line, or by the upstream rate limit enforced by a cable modem. The CPE is typically unaware of these rates and sends at the speed of its physical link.¶

In the upstream direction, the provisioned rate is typically enforced at the BNG on traffic received from the subscriber. Per-subscriber queuing in the upstream direction is not supported by all BNG platforms. Where it is supported, it is often restricted to specific hardware, subject to additional constraints, or available only at reduced scale, as it competes for the same queuing and scheduling resources as downstream hierarchical QoS. Upstream enforcement is therefore commonly implemented using ingress policers. A policer does not queue packets; it discards all packets that exceed the configured rate and burst size. As the CPE sends at the speed of its physical link, which can exceed the provisioned rate by an order of magnitude, traffic bursts are discarded at the policer. Congestion control algorithms react to these losses by repeatedly reducing their sending rate, resulting in an unstable sawtooth throughput pattern, and the achieved throughput often remains well below the provisioned rate. Users experience this as poor and fluctuating results, for example in speed tests, even though no part of the network is congested.¶

Where the upstream bottleneck is located at the access device or within the access network, the resulting queue is typically managed by neither AQM nor prioritization of the subscriber's traffic. Depending on the buffering of the access device, this results in packet loss, with effects similar to those of a policer, or in increased latency under load. In both cases, all traffic of the subscriber is affected alike, including latency-sensitive applications.¶

Many CPE implementations support upstream prioritization, for example of DNS, Voice over IP (VoIP), online gaming, or video conferencing traffic, of TCP acknowledgments, or of selected clients or applications. Such prioritization is only effective if the CPE is the bottleneck of the upstream path. As long as the CPE sends at the speed of its physical link, its queues remain empty, and packets are discarded or delayed at the actual bottleneck, such as the policer of the BNG or the access device, regardless of their priority.¶

In the downstream direction, the provisioned rate is typically enforced at the BNG using egress shapers, where per-subscriber queuing is widely available. The downstream rate is therefore of lower relevance for traffic management at the CPE, although a CPE can use it to control downstream queuing delay where the shaper at the BNG does not apply AQM. It is, however, relevant for intermediate nodes that apply per-subscriber traffic management in both directions, and it is useful for informational purposes, for example to display the provisioned service tier in the user interface of the CPE. For this purpose, the option also allows the Advertised Rates to be signaled separately (see Section 3.2.1).¶

A similar lack of visibility exists within local area networks (LANs), such as home, enterprise, or public Wi-Fi hotspot networks, where end devices are typically connected at link speeds that exceed the capacity of the upstream connection (see Section 7.2).¶

1.2. Solution Overview

This document defines new DHCP options for both DHCPv4 and DHCPv6 to signal the available upstream and downstream rates. If the CPE knows the available upstream rate, it can shape its upstream traffic to this rate, regardless of where the bottleneck is located. The queue then builds at the CPE, where it can be managed using prioritization, Active Queue Management (AQM) [RFC7567], and flow queuing [RFC8290]. Under normal operation, the policer at the BNG is not triggered, and the access device is not congested (see Figure 1). The resulting upstream throughput is stable and close to the available rate, and latency under load remains low. When combined with the Low Latency, Low Loss, and Scalable Throughput (L4S) architecture [RFC9330], the queue at the CPE can additionally provide low latency for L4S traffic.¶

The enforcement at the BNG remains in place. The shaper at the CPE does not replace this enforcement but prevents it from being triggered. CPE that do not support the options defined in this document continue to be subject to the enforcement at the BNG, which can be configured slightly above the signaled rate for this purpose (see Section 3.4).¶

The same rate information can be used by intermediate nodes. In many IP over Ethernet (IPoE) [TR101] architectures, the BNG operates as a DHCP relay agent. While per-subscriber traffic management policies are typically provisioned via Authentication, Authorization, and Accounting (AAA) protocols such as RADIUS [RFC2865], deployments lacking AAA integration at the service edge require an in-band alternative. The BNG can use the signaled rates to instantiate downstream shapers and upstream policers, and access nodes performing DHCP snooping can use them to configure their local queues and shapers. DHCPv6 additionally allows the server to signal different rates to the client and to each relay agent in the path (see Section 5.3).¶

Where the actual rate of the access line is lower than the provisioned rate, for example due to the synchronization rate of a DSL line, the server can signal the lower of the two rates. The actual rate of the access line is typically known at the BNG, for example from access loop characteristics reported by the access node [TR101], such as the Actual-Data-Rate-Upstream and Actual-Data-Rate-Downstream attributes [RFC4679] or the corresponding Access Node Control Protocol (ANCP) TLVs [RFC6320] (see Appendix C).¶

1.3. Applicability

The mechanism defined in this document is primarily designed for broadband access networks using IPoE or Point-to-Point Protocol over Ethernet (PPPoE) [RFC2516]. In PPPoE deployments, DHCPv6 is commonly used over the PPP session for IPv6 Prefix Delegation and conveys the options in the same way (see Section 7.1). The options are defined for both DHCPv4 and DHCPv6 to support IPv4-only, IPv6-only, and dual-stack deployments. A minimal implementation, which supports only the upstream rate and its Applied Rate Layer, is described in Section 3.5.¶

Unlike auto-configuration protocols such as TR-069 [TR069], which are not deployed by all operators and are typically not available for customer-owned CPE, DHCP is supported by virtually all CPE, including retail routers and devices running open-source firmware (e.g., OpenWrt), and it is visible to intermediate nodes along the path. Rates conveyed via DHCP are refreshed with every lease renewal and can be updated dynamically. The options are also applicable within LANs (see Section 7.2).¶

Appendix A discusses the reasoning behind this mechanism and addresses frequently raised questions, such as why rate enforcement at the BNG alone is not sufficient and why upstream shaping is not performed at the BNG.¶

3. DHCP Rate Option

The DHCP Rate Option (OPTION_RATE) specified in this document is a container option with a sub-option structure for both DHCPv4 and DHCPv6. This document uses OPTION_RATE to refer to both the DHCPv4 option (OPTION_V4_RATE) and the DHCPv6 option (OPTION_V6_RATE). The top-level option encapsulation conforms to the requirements of each base protocol. Specifically, DHCPv4 uses an 8-bit option code set to TBD1 alongside an 8-bit length field, whereas DHCPv6 uses a 16-bit option code set to TBD2 alongside a 16-bit length field.¶

While the underlying semantics of the rate information remain identical across both IP versions, the internal payload sizing differs to align with the conventions of each respective protocol. For DHCPv4, the encapsulated sub-options use an 8-bit sub-option code and an 8-bit sub-option length field, mirroring the format of DHCPv4 Option 82 (Relay Agent Information Option) [RFC3046]. Conversely, for DHCPv6, the encapsulated sub-options use a 16-bit sub-option code and a 16-bit sub-option length field.¶

Section 9 of [RFC7227] favors encapsulated options, which use the DHCPv6 option code space, over sub-options with an option-specific code space. This document nevertheless uses sub-options with a code space shared by DHCPv4 and DHCPv6, so that a single registry and identical semantics apply to both protocols. This simplifies implementations supporting both protocols and ensures consistent processing in dual-stack deployments (see Section 3.4). The sub-options are defined on a single level only and, for those defined in this document, have a fixed length.¶

3.2. Sub-Options

3.2.1. Advertised Rates

The Advertised Rate Upstream (code 1) and Advertised Rate Downstream (code 2) sub-options convey the Advertised Rates (see Section 2), for example the rates of the subscribed service as specified in the service agreement, which are typically specified as payload rates. In local area networks, they can convey the nominal capacity available to the clients (see Section 7.2). Their format is identical to the format of the Applied Rate sub-options (see Section 3.2.2), using the sub-option codes 1 and 2 and a sub-option length of 8. The rate is a 64-bit unsigned integer in bits per second (bps) in network byte order.¶

Advertised Rates are informational only. Receivers SHOULD NOT apply them to traffic management functions, unless explicitly configured otherwise by local policy. They are not subject to the Applied Rate Layer or the Per-Packet Overhead sub-options. The layer to which an Advertised Rate refers is not specified; it reflects the rate as communicated to the subscriber. Receivers can use them, for example, to populate user interfaces, for telemetry, as an initial estimate for applications, or as a metric to select a path with higher throughput. A value of 0, or the absence of the sub-option, indicates that no Advertised Rate is provided for the respective direction. Relay agents and snooping devices do not use Advertised Rates for their traffic management.¶

Advertised Rates can differ from the Applied Rates. For example, an operator can signal an Applied Rate accounted at Layer 2, configured such that the payload rate achieved with maximum-sized packets meets the contracted rate, together with the contracted rate as the Advertised Rate (see Section 8).¶

3.2.4. Applied Rate Layer

The Applied Rate Layer indicates the layer at which the enforcement point accounts traffic, and thereby the layer to which the Applied Rates apply, so that receivers can model this accounting.¶

Two sub-options with identical format and semantics are defined. The Applied Rate Layer sub-option (code 5) applies to both directions. If the Applied Rate Layer Downstream sub-option (code 6) is present, it overrides the Applied Rate Layer sub-option for the downstream direction. This allows a server, for example, to signal rates accounted at different layers in each direction. Throughout this document, the Applied Rate Layer of a direction refers to the Applied Rate Layer applicable to that direction.¶

A Layer 2 rate encompasses the Ethernet frame as transmitted on the link over which the receiver is connected to the network, including the Ethernet header, all Virtual LAN (VLAN) tags [IEEE802.1Q] present on that link, and the complete Ethernet payload, including any encapsulation headers carried within it (e.g., PPPoE and PPP headers). It excludes the Ethernet Frame Check Sequence (FCS), preamble, start frame delimiter (SFD), and inter-packet gap (IPG) [IEEE802.3]. For link layers other than Ethernet, the Layer 2 rate is calculated analogously over the link-layer header and payload, excluding link-layer trailers and physical-layer framing overhead.¶

A Layer 3 rate encompasses only the IP header and its payload. This rate calculation is frequently used by end-user speed test applications and is often regarded as the marketable "product bandwidth". Its primary advantage is its independence from the variable overhead introduced by differing numbers of VLAN tags or tunnel encapsulations, which makes it suitable for comparison with end-user measurements.¶

The Layer 2 rate refers to the frames on the link of the receiver, independent of the logical interface on which the receiver applies its traffic management functions. A receiver that applies traffic management functions on a logical interface on which the frames differ from those on the link, such as a VLAN interface whose tags are added by an underlying device, or a PPP interface, accounts for the difference locally, for example by adjusting the per-packet overhead of its traffic management functions. The per-packet overhead can also differ between the link of the receiver and the enforcement point, for example when an access node adds or removes a VLAN tag. Servers can signal such differences using the Per-Packet Overhead sub-options (see Section 3.2.5) or, in DHCPv6, by signaling node-specific rates (see Section 5.3).¶

For a direction for which no Applied Rate Layer sub-option applies, a receiver MUST interpret the Applied Rate as a Layer 3 rate. The Layer 3 default corresponds to the rate measured by end-user speed tests and to existing deployments of proprietary rate signaling mechanisms.¶

Values other than 2 and 3 are invalid, and no registry is defined for this field. Other accounting models can be defined as additional sub-options (see Table 5). If a receiver receives an Applied Rate Layer sub-option with an invalid value, it does not apply the Applied Rate of that direction and treats it as information only (see Section 3.3). The remaining sub-options are processed as usual (see Section 3.8).¶

Servers SHOULD signal the Applied Rates at the layer at which the enforcement point accounts traffic and SHOULD include the Per-Packet Overhead sub-options whenever the accounting of the enforcement point is known, including when all of their values are 0. A Layer 2 rate together with the per-packet overhead allows the most precise modeling of a bottleneck that accounts traffic at Layer 2. A Layer 3 rate is useful where the enforcement point itself accounts traffic at Layer 3, or where the effective rate is intended to match the rates measured by end-user speed tests.¶

A receiver whose traffic management functions account traffic at Layer 3 can apply a Layer 3 rate directly. The effective rate then closely corresponds to the rate measured by end-user speed tests and is identical on every node, independent of the encapsulation used on the link of the receiver or on any other segment of the path.¶

The traffic management functions of a receiver do not necessarily account traffic at the layer indicated by the Applied Rate Layer. For example, shapers commonly account for the Layer 2 frame length, while the signaled rate can be a Layer 3 rate. A receiver whose traffic management functions support per-packet overhead accounting compensates for the difference between its own accounting and the accounting of the enforcement point, as indicated by the Applied Rate Layer and the Per-Packet Overhead sub-options (see Section 3.2.5). For a Layer 3 rate, this includes the headers between Layer 3 and its own accounting layer, such as Ethernet, VLAN, and PPPoE headers, which are known locally, for example by configuring a negative overhead, so that the effective rate corresponds to the signaled Layer 3 rate. A receiver without such support applies the signaled rate directly or approximates the difference, for example by reducing the rate by a margin.¶

A client MAY indicate its preferred layer as a hint (see Section 3.6).¶

3.2.5. Per-Packet Overhead

The Per-Packet Overhead sub-options describe the size that the enforcement point of the signaled rates accounts for each packet, relative to the size of the packet at the layer indicated by the Applied Rate Layer of the respective direction, on the link of the receiver. They allow a receiver to configure its traffic management functions such that they accurately model the enforcement point, independent of differences in encapsulation between the receiver and the enforcement point, such as additional VLAN tags or cell-based link layers.¶

Two sub-options with identical format and semantics are defined. The Per-Packet Overhead sub-option (code 7) applies to both directions. If the Per-Packet Overhead Downstream sub-option (code 8) is present, it overrides the Per-Packet Overhead sub-option for the downstream direction. As the per-packet overhead is typically identical in both directions, and clients typically apply only the upstream rate (see Section 3.3), servers usually include only the Per-Packet Overhead sub-option. As the Overhead is relative to the layer indicated by the Applied Rate Layer of the respective direction, a server that signals different Applied Rate Layers for the two directions includes a Per-Packet Overhead Downstream sub-option where the Overhead differs as a result.¶

The format is shown for the Per-Packet Overhead sub-option. The Per-Packet Overhead Downstream sub-option uses the same format with a sub-option code of 8.¶

¶

Overhead:

A 16-bit signed integer in two's complement and network byte order, indicating the number of octets the enforcement point accounts per packet in addition to the size of the packet at the layer indicated by the Applied Rate Layer of the respective direction, on the link of the receiver. The value can be negative, for example if the enforcement point does not account for headers that are present on the link of the receiver. Where the bottleneck is a physical link rather than a policer, the Overhead includes physical-layer overhead that consumes transmission time, such as the Ethernet preamble, SFD, and IPG.¶

Minimum Packet Unit (MPU):

A 16-bit unsigned integer in network byte order, indicating the minimum size in octets that the enforcement point accounts per packet, after the Overhead has been added. A value of 0 indicates that no minimum applies.¶

Cell Payload:

An 8-bit unsigned integer indicating the number of octets of packet data carried per cell for link layers that transport packets in fixed-size cells. A value of 0 indicates that no cell-based accounting applies.¶

Cell Size:

An 8-bit unsigned integer indicating the number of octets accounted per cell, including the cell header. If the Cell Payload is 0, the Cell Size MUST be set to 0 by the sender and is ignored by the receiver.¶

For a packet with a size of S octets at the layer indicated by the Applied Rate Layer of the respective direction, the size L accounted by the enforcement point is calculated as follows:¶

  1. L = max(S + Overhead, MPU)¶

  2. If the Cell Payload is not 0: L = ceil(L / Cell Payload) * Cell Size¶

Cell-based accounting describes link layers that pad each packet to an integer number of cells. The most common example is Asynchronous Transfer Mode (ATM) Adaptation Layer 5 (AAL5), as used for example on Asymmetric Digital Subscriber Line (ADSL), ADSL2, and ADSL2+ lines, which is described by a Cell Payload of 48 and a Cell Size of 53. In this case, the Overhead includes all encapsulation overhead carried within the AAL5 frame, including the 8-octet AAL5 trailer, and the signaled rate corresponds to the rate of ATM cells, for example the synchronization rate of the ADSL line. A Cell Size smaller than the Cell Payload is invalid; a receiver that receives such values ignores the cell-based accounting. Accounting models that cannot be described by these parameters can be defined as additional sub-options (see Table 5). Examples of parameter values for common deployments are given in Appendix B.¶

The Per-Packet Overhead sub-options are optional and supplement the signaled rates to improve the accuracy of traffic management. A receiver that applies the signaled rates and whose traffic management functions support per-packet overhead accounting SHOULD configure them such that the rate of the traffic in the respective direction, calculated over the accounted sizes L, does not exceed the signaled rate for that direction.¶

A receiver whose traffic management functions do not support per-packet overhead accounting, or support it only partially (e.g., without cell-based accounting or only for a limited range of values), MAY ignore the sub-options or those parts of them that it cannot apply, and applies the signaled rates as specified in Section 3.2.4. Such a receiver MAY reduce the rate it applies by a margin to approximate the accounting of the enforcement point. The inability of a receiver to apply the Per-Packet Overhead sub-options, fully or partially, does not render the OPTION_RATE invalid.¶

For a direction for which no Per-Packet Overhead sub-option applies, the server does not provide information about the accounting of the enforcement point. In this case, the receiver uses its local configuration, if any, or otherwise assumes an Overhead of 0, an MPU of 0, and no cell-based accounting. If a Per-Packet Overhead sub-option applies to a direction, its values take precedence over the default values of the receiver for that direction, including values of 0, which indicate that the enforcement point does not account any additional overhead. Explicit configuration by the administrator of the receiver takes precedence over the signaled values (see Section 3.3).¶

3.3. Traffic Management Using Applied Rates

The Applied Rate sub-options convey rates that are intended to be applied to local traffic management functions, such as traffic shapers, policers, or AQM parameters, at the layer indicated by the Applied Rate Layer (see Section 3.2.4). A client that supports such traffic management functions SHOULD apply the upstream rate and MAY apply the downstream rate. Relay agents and snooping devices MAY apply the rates in either direction (see Section 4, Section 5, and Section 6).¶

From the perspective of a client, the upstream rate is the relevant one: downstream traffic is typically already shaped by the network before it reaches the client, whereas upstream traffic is enforced by the network only after it has left the client. A client therefore typically applies only the upstream rate to its traffic management functions, which is why applying the downstream rate is optional for clients. Where the downstream shaper at the BNG does not apply AQM, a client can additionally shape received traffic slightly below the downstream rate to control queuing delay. Intermediate nodes, such as relay agents and snooping devices, typically apply the rates in both directions.¶

A receiver that does not apply an Applied Rate, for example because it does not support the required traffic management functions, because its processing capacity is not sufficient to apply traffic management at the signaled rate, or because applying the downstream rate is optional for clients, treats the rate as information only. In particular, such a receiver can expose the rate in its management interfaces (see Section 8).¶

The presence of an Applied Rate sub-option always indicates a rate that is intended to be applied. A server that intends to provide rate information for informational purposes only, without the rates being applied, uses the Advertised Rate sub-options (see Section 3.2.1) instead of the Applied Rate sub-options.¶

Explicit configuration by the administrator of the receiver, such as disabling the application of signaled rates or configuring fixed rates or overhead values, takes precedence over the signaled values. In the absence of such configuration, the signaled values take precedence over the default values of the receiver.¶

3.4. Rate Processing

The processing of the DHCP Rate Option is intentionally stateless with respect to the DHCP protocol version and the DHCP lease, such that it can be implemented by simple event-driven scripts on the client. A receiver only needs to retain the rates currently in effect.¶

Rates are maintained per link. DHCPv4 and DHCPv6 messages received over the same link share the rates in effect, even if they are processed on different logical interfaces of the receiver. The rules in this section apply independently to each link on which an OPTION_RATE is received.¶

Each valid OPTION_RATE received in a DHCPv4 DHCPACK message or a DHCPv6 Reply message (or, for DHCPv6 relay agents, in the corresponding Relay-reply message) replaces the rates previously in effect in their entirety, regardless of whether the previous rates were learned via DHCPv4 or DHCPv6:¶

  • If an Applied Rate sub-option is present, the receiver applies the signaled rate for the corresponding direction as specified in Section 3.3.¶

  • If an Applied Rate sub-option is absent, or carries a value of 0, the receiver MUST remove any rate previously applied for the corresponding direction and revert to its default configuration for that direction.¶

  • Advertised Rate sub-options replace any previously received Advertised Rates. If an Advertised Rate sub-option is absent, no Advertised Rate is provided for the respective direction.¶

Consequently, an OPTION_RATE without any sub-options instructs the receiver to revert to its default configuration for both directions. Because some DHCPv4 client implementations discard options with a length of zero, servers SHOULD NOT send an OPTION_RATE without sub-options in DHCPv4. To remove previously signaled rates for both directions, a server instead includes an OPTION_RATE containing Applied Rate sub-options with a value of 0. These semantics apply to an OPTION_RATE received from a server; an OPTION_RATE without sub-options sent by a relay agent in a Relay-forward message indicates support for this option (see Section 5.4).¶

If the rates, Applied Rate Layers, and per-packet overhead contained in a valid OPTION_RATE are identical to the currently applied ones, the receiver SHOULD NOT reconfigure its traffic management functions.¶

If a DHCPACK or Reply message does not contain an OPTION_RATE, the receiver MUST retain the rates currently in effect unchanged.¶

An OPTION_RATE that is discarded in accordance with Section 3.8 is treated as if it were not present, and the rates currently in effect remain unchanged.¶

For clients, the rates in effect are not bound to the lifetime of the DHCP lease from which they were learned. Relay agents and snooping devices remove the rates applied for a client together with the corresponding binding (see Section 4, Section 5.4, and Section 6). A receiver MUST revert to its default configuration when the underlying link or session (e.g., the PPPoE session) is terminated.¶

Relay agents and snooping devices apply signaled rates to the traffic of an individual client. This document assumes that each such client is associated with a dedicated physical or logical client-facing port, such as an access line or a per-client VLAN (e.g., the 1:1 VLAN model described in [TR101]). If multiple clients share the same client-facing port, and the device is not able to apply rates to the traffic of each client individually, the device SHOULD NOT apply the signaled rates to that port.¶

Intermediate nodes that enforce the signaled rates MAY apply an implementation-specific correction factor, for example by enforcing a rate slightly above the signaled rate (e.g., by 1%). This allows a client that supports this option to shape its traffic to the signaled rate without triggering the enforcement at the intermediate node, while the intermediate node still enforces the provisioned rate for clients that do not support or ignore this option. Alternatively, in DHCPv6, the server can signal node-specific rates (see Section 5.3).¶

A client can receive an OPTION_RATE indicating an Applied Rate that exceeds the maximum physical link speed of its upstream or downstream interfaces (e.g., signaling 2 Gbit/s to a CPE with a 1 Gbit/s Wide Area Network (WAN) port). In such scenarios, the client SHOULD cap the applied traffic shaping, policing, or AQM parameters to the maximum capacity of the physical link.¶

In dual-stack deployments, servers SHOULD either signal identical rate information via both DHCPv4 and DHCPv6, or signal rate information via only one of the two protocols that is expected to be negotiated by every client. In the latter case, messages of the other protocol do not carry an OPTION_RATE and therefore leave the rates in effect unchanged. If neither approach is followed, the rates in effect depend on which message was received last.¶

3.5. Minimal Implementation

The DHCP Rate Option is designed such that receivers can implement a subset of its sub-options. A minimal client implementation supports the Applied Rate Upstream and Applied Rate Layer sub-options, applies the upstream rate at the layer indicated by its Applied Rate Layer, and implements the rules specified in Section 3.4 and Section 3.8. Such an implementation MAY treat sub-options that it does not implement, such as the Per-Packet Overhead or Advertised Rate sub-options, as unrecognized sub-option codes (see Section 3.8). Support for the remaining sub-options improves the accuracy and flexibility of traffic management but is not required for interoperability.¶

3.6. Client Hints

A client MAY include an OPTION_RATE in its messages to the server as a hint. Hints reuse the formats of the Applied Rate and Applied Rate Layer sub-options, with the semantics defined in this section. In a client message, the sub-options have the following meaning:¶

  • An Applied Rate sub-option indicates the maximum rate the client is able to handle in the corresponding direction, for example due to the capacity of its physical interface. It does not request a specific service rate.¶

  • The Applied Rate Layer sub-option indicates the layer preferred by the client. A client MAY include an Applied Rate Layer Downstream sub-option to indicate a different preference for the downstream direction.¶

  • An absent sub-option, or a rate value of 0, indicates that the client has no preference or limitation for the corresponding direction.¶

  • Clients do not include Per-Packet Overhead or Advertised Rate sub-options in hints; servers ignore them if present.¶

The rules specified in Section 3.4 do not apply to hints. A client MAY include hints in DHCPDISCOVER, DHCPREQUEST, and DHCPINFORM messages for DHCPv4, and in Solicit, Request, Renew, Rebind, and Information-request messages for DHCPv6. A client that includes hints SHOULD include the same hints in all of these messages.¶

Sending hints is OPTIONAL for the client, and honoring them is OPTIONAL for the server. A server that honors a hint uses it to limit the signaled rates to the capacity indicated by the client, i.e., it signals the lower of the provisioned rate and the hinted rate. How a server otherwise processes hints is implementation specific. The server remains authoritative: the client MUST apply the rate information provided by the server in accordance with Section 3.4, regardless of any hint it sent.¶

Hints are addressed to the server only. Relay agents and snooping devices MUST NOT use an OPTION_RATE contained in a client message to configure their traffic management. As intermediate nodes can derive their enforcement from the signaled rates, honoring a hint can also lower the enforcement in the network for that client. As the resulting rate never exceeds the provisioned rate, this only affects the client that sent the hint.¶

3.8. Error Handling

Conflicting rate information received via DHCPv4 and DHCPv6 is resolved as specified in Section 3.4: the most recently received valid OPTION_RATE takes precedence.¶

To ensure forward compatibility, receivers and servers MUST ignore unrecognized sub-option codes and continue processing the remainder of the OPTION_RATE.¶

If a receiver processes a recognized sub-option containing an unrecognized value, it handles the value as specified for the respective sub-option. For an invalid Applied Rate Layer value, the receiver does not apply the Applied Rate of the affected direction, as applying a rate without understanding its accounting could result in incorrect traffic management (see Section 3.2.4). Values of the Per-Packet Overhead sub-options that a receiver cannot apply do not affect the processing of the remaining sub-options (see Section 3.2.5). Specifications defining additional sub-options specify the handling of unrecognized values.¶

A receiver MUST also discard the entire OPTION_RATE if any of the following conditions is met:¶

  • the length of a recognized sub-option differs from the length specified for that sub-option;¶

  • the length of a sub-option exceeds the number of octets remaining in the OPTION_RATE;¶

  • the number of octets remaining in the OPTION_RATE is non-zero but smaller than the size of a sub-option header (2 octets for DHCPv4, 4 octets for DHCPv6).¶

A discarded OPTION_RATE is treated as if it were not present (see Section 3.4).¶

A sender MUST NOT include more than one OPTION_RATE in a DHCPv4 message, or more than one OPTION_RATE at the same encapsulation level of a DHCPv6 message (i.e., within the message exchanged with the client or within a given Relay-reply message). A sender MUST NOT include the same sub-option more than once within an OPTION_RATE.¶

If a DHCPv4 receiver nevertheless receives multiple instances of OPTION_RATE, it MUST concatenate them as specified in [RFC3396] and process the result as a single OPTION_RATE. If a DHCPv6 receiver receives more than one OPTION_RATE at the same encapsulation level, it SHOULD discard all instances, which are then treated as if they were not present (see Section 3.4).¶

If multiple instances of the same sub-option code are present within an OPTION_RATE, the last instance MUST be processed.¶

4.2. DHCPv4 Client Behavior

DHCPv4 clients that support the DHCP Rate Option SHOULD include the corresponding OPTION_RATE code in the Parameter Request List (PRL). This inclusion signals client support, enabling the DHCP server to determine whether to include the rate parameters in its response. Furthermore, this signaling provides network operators with visibility into client capabilities, which aids in troubleshooting and resolving customer service quality complaints.¶

A client MAY include hints as specified in Section 3.6. For example, when a CPE is connected via a 1 Gbit/s WAN interface to an external ONT or modem capable of exceeding 1 Gbit/s on the WAN side, the CPE can signal this physical limitation to the service provider. This enables the network to align its shaping parameters with the device's actual capacity, ensuring traffic does not exceed the physical limit.¶

A client MUST NOT apply rates received in any DHCPv4 message other than DHCPACK. A client MAY use the rate information contained in a DHCPOFFER message as a criterion for selecting among multiple offers. Rates received in a DHCPACK message are processed as specified in Section 3.4.¶

4.3. DHCPv4 Server Behavior

When a DHCPv4 server that supports the DHCP Rate Option receives a client message whose PRL includes OPTION_RATE, and rate information is configured for the client, the server MUST include an OPTION_RATE reflecting the current rate information in the resulting DHCPOFFER and DHCPACK messages. If no rate information is configured for the client, the server MAY omit the OPTION_RATE. Because a missing OPTION_RATE leaves the rates in effect unchanged, a server that needs to remove previously signaled rates MUST include an OPTION_RATE without the respective Applied Rate sub-options or with a rate value of 0, as specified in Section 3.4.¶

The server MAY derive the specific upstream and downstream rates and Applied Rate Layers from local configuration profiles, centralized AAA systems such as RADIUS, or external policy servers.¶

A server MAY include the OPTION_RATE in its responses even if the client did not request it via the PRL, provided the operator has explicitly configured the server to do so, for example to provision Layer 2 DHCPv4 Relay Agents or snooping devices.¶

To update the rates of a client before its next scheduled renewal, a server MAY send a DHCPFORCERENEW message [RFC3203], causing the client to initiate a renewal transaction. DHCPFORCERENEW messages are subject to the authentication requirements of [RFC3203], which can be satisfied using the mechanism specified in [RFC6704]. For clients that do not support DHCPFORCERENEW, updated rates take effect with the next renewal, the timing of which is controlled by the renewal (T1) time value.¶

4.4. DHCPv4 Relay Agent Behavior

DHCPv4 relay agents, including Layer 2 DHCPv4 Relay Agents [TR101], MAY extract the OPTION_RATE from DHCPACK messages traversing the network. Relay agents that perform localized traffic management MAY use these extracted values to dynamically instantiate shapers and policers on their client-facing interfaces. Relay agents SHOULD remove rates applied for a client when the corresponding binding is removed. Layer 2 DHCPv4 Relay Agents MUST only process an OPTION_RATE contained in DHCPACK messages received on trusted, network-facing ports.¶

In many architectures, the BNG or a similar device acts as the authoritative policy enforcement point. Modifying the message destined for the client is outside the relay agent behavior specified in [RFC2131] and [RFC3046]. A device that populates, modifies, or removes the OPTION_RATE destined for the client based on subscriber attributes (e.g., retrieved from an AAA server such as RADIUS) therefore acts as a DHCPv4 server or DHCPv4 proxy towards the client, and the server behavior specified in this document applies to it.¶

5.2. DHCPv6 Client Behavior

DHCPv6 clients that support the DHCP Rate Option SHOULD include the corresponding OPTION_RATE code in the Option Request option (ORO) [RFC9915]. This inclusion signals client support, enabling the DHCPv6 server to determine whether to include the rate parameters in its response. Furthermore, this signaling provides network operators with visibility into client capabilities, which aids in troubleshooting and resolving customer service quality complaints.¶

A client MAY include hints as specified in Section 3.6.¶

A client MUST NOT apply rates received in any DHCPv6 message other than Reply. A client MAY use the rate information contained in an Advertise message as a criterion for selecting among multiple Advertise messages. Rates received in a Reply message are processed as specified in Section 3.4.¶

5.3. DHCPv6 Server Behavior

When a DHCPv6 server that supports the DHCP Rate Option receives a Solicit, Request, Renew, Rebind, or Information-request message whose ORO includes OPTION_RATE, and rate information is configured for the client, the server MUST include an OPTION_RATE reflecting the current rate information in the resulting Advertise and Reply messages. If no rate information is configured for the client, the server MAY omit the OPTION_RATE. Because a missing OPTION_RATE leaves the rates in effect unchanged, a server that needs to remove previously signaled rates MUST include an OPTION_RATE without the respective Applied Rate sub-options or with a rate value of 0, as specified in Section 3.4.¶

A server MAY include the OPTION_RATE in its responses even if the client did not request it via the ORO, provided the operator has explicitly configured the server to do so.¶

In relayed exchanges, the OPTION_RATE for the client is included in the Reply message, which is encapsulated in one or more Relay-reply messages. The server MAY additionally include the option in a Relay-reply message to provision the corresponding relay agent. The nested Relay-reply structure of DHCPv6 allows the server to address each relay agent in the path individually, in addition to the client.¶

DHCPv6 does not define a mechanism for relay agents to request options from the server. A server therefore includes the OPTION_RATE in a Relay-reply message based on local configuration, for example by identifying the relay agent by the link-address or peer-address field of the Relay-forward message, or by relay agent options such as Interface-Id [RFC9915] or Remote-Id [RFC4649]. A server MAY additionally take into account a support indication received from the relay agent (see Section 5.4).¶

If network policy dictates localized traffic management at both the CPE and the relay node, the server MAY include the OPTION_RATE at all encapsulation levels simultaneously. When provisioning multiple levels, the server MAY supply different rate values to each respective node. For example, an operator might configure a relay agent's upstream policer with a slightly higher rate limit than the CPE's upstream shaper. This operational delta accommodates minor traffic burstiness from the CPE and prevents premature packet drops at the intermediate access node. DHCPv4 does not provide a comparable mechanism for addressing multiple relay agents individually.¶

To update the rates of a client before its next scheduled renewal, a server MAY send a Reconfigure message as specified in Section 18.3.11 of [RFC9915], causing the client to initiate a Renew, Rebind, or Information-request transaction. As specified in [RFC9915], a server can only send Reconfigure messages to clients that have included the Reconfigure Accept option in their messages, and Reconfigure messages are authenticated using the Reconfiguration Key Authentication Protocol (RKAP; see Section 20.4 of [RFC9915]).¶

For clients that do not support Reconfigure, updated rates take effect with the next Renew or Rebind transaction or, for stateless clients, with the next Information-request transaction. Operators can control the timing of such updates through the T1 value and the Information Refresh Time option (Section 21.23 of [RFC9915]).¶

5.4. DHCPv6 Relay Agent Behavior

DHCPv6 relay agents that support the DHCP Rate Option, including Lightweight DHCPv6 Relay Agents (LDRA) [RFC6221], process an OPTION_RATE contained in a Relay-reply message addressed to them as specified in Section 3.4. Such relay agents MAY use the signaled rates to instantiate shapers, policers, or AQM disciplines on their client-facing interfaces. Relay agents SHOULD remove rates applied for a client when the corresponding binding is removed. Relay agents MUST only process Relay-reply messages received from the network side; in particular, an LDRA discards Relay-reply messages received on client-facing interfaces as specified in Section 6.1.1 of [RFC6221].¶

A relay agent that supports the DHCP Rate Option MAY include an OPTION_RATE with a length of 0 in its Relay-forward messages to indicate its support to the server. A server MUST NOT interpret such an option as a rate hint.¶

An OPTION_RATE contained in the encapsulated message destined for the client targets the client only. Relay agents MUST NOT use such an option to configure their own traffic management, since the DHCPv6 relay architecture provides the Relay-reply message as a dedicated channel for signaling rates to relay agents.¶

For an OPTION_RATE contained in a Relay-reply message, the Layer 2 rate and the Per-Packet Overhead sub-options refer to the frames on the client-facing link of the relay agent (see Section 3.2.4 and Section 3.2.5), which can differ from the frames as seen by the client.¶

In many architectures, the BNG or a similar device acts as the authoritative policy enforcement point. Modifying the message destined for the client is outside the relay agent behavior specified in [RFC9915]. A device that populates, modifies, or removes the OPTION_RATE destined for the client based on subscriber attributes (e.g., retrieved from an AAA server such as RADIUS) therefore acts as a DHCPv6 server or DHCPv6 proxy towards the client, and the server behavior specified in this document applies to it.¶

6. DHCP Snooping

DHCP snooping switches are typically deployed as intermediate Layer 2 devices that passively monitor DHCP message exchanges to enforce security policies and build binding databases, all without modifying the DHCP payloads. These devices MAY passively inspect the DHCP Rate Option within messages destined for clients. By extracting these rate parameters, snooping devices can dynamically provision appropriate traffic shapers, policers, or hardware queues on the corresponding downstream, client-facing ports. Snooping devices SHOULD remove rates applied for a client when the corresponding binding is removed.¶

Snooping devices MUST only process an OPTION_RATE contained in DHCPACK (DHCPv4) and Reply (DHCPv6) messages received on trusted, network-facing ports. An OPTION_RATE contained in messages received on client-facing ports MUST NOT be used to configure traffic management.¶

7. Deployment Scenarios

This section describes the application of the DHCP Rate Option in specific deployment scenarios.¶

7.1. PPPoE

In PPPoE [RFC2516] architectures, the CPE typically employs DHCPv6 over the PPP [RFC1661] link to obtain an IPv6 prefix via Prefix Delegation (IA_PD) [RFC9915]. This encapsulated DHCPv6 exchange provides a standardized transport mechanism for the DHCP Rate Option. While less prevalent in modern deployments, DHCPv4 transactions operating within a PPPoE session can similarly convey the DHCP Rate Option.¶

The processing rules and client behavior for an OPTION_RATE received over PPPoE are identical to those defined for IPoE environments, as specified in Section 3.4. In particular, the rates in effect revert to the default configuration of the device when the underlying PPPoE session is terminated.¶

If a client receives rate information embedded within a PPP authentication message and also receives an OPTION_RATE, the client SHOULD give precedence to the OPTION_RATE. This ensures that the standardized, dynamic DHCP signaling supersedes proprietary rate information conveyed during the PPP authentication phase.¶

In PPPoE deployments, the OPTION_RATE is received on the PPP interface, while a Layer 2 rate refers to the Ethernet frames on the link, including the PPPoE and PPP headers (see Section 3.2.4). A receiver that applies its traffic management functions to the PPP interface accounts for the Ethernet, PPPoE, and PPP headers as per-packet overhead.¶

7.2. Local Area Networks

The DHCP Rate Option can also be used by DHCP servers in local area networks, for example a DHCP server co-located with a CPE or another gateway, to inform clients about the rates available to them. In this case, the signaled rates indicate the maximum rate a client can expect through the network it is attached to. Examples include:¶

  • Virtual Private Network (VPN) gateways or tunnel endpoints that shape traffic towards their peers and whose processing capacity exceeds the capacity of the upstream connection.¶

  • Public Wi-Fi hotspots that enforce per-user rate limits, where clients can align their sending rate with the enforced limit to avoid policer drops.¶

  • Applications on end devices that use the signaled rates as an initial estimate, e.g., to estimate transfer times, to select an initial video quality, or to choose between available network interfaces.¶

A device that acts as a DHCP client on its network-facing interface and as a DHCP server on its LAN interfaces MAY derive the rates it signals to LAN clients from the rates learned on its network-facing interface, from local configuration, or both. In this case, it SHOULD signal the lower of the learned rate and any locally configured limit for the client. When the learned rates change, the device SHOULD signal the updated rates to its LAN clients with their next renewal and MAY trigger an earlier update as specified in Section 4.3 and Section 5.3.¶

Unless the signaled rates are enforced for each client individually, they describe a capacity shared by all clients of the LAN. In this case, the server SHOULD signal them using the Advertised Rate sub-options (see Section 3.2.1) rather than the Applied Rate sub-options, so that clients use the rates as information rather than configuring traffic management functions based on them.¶

Clients cap the effective rates to the capacity of their physical link (see Section 3.4), for example when a client is connected at 1 Gbit/s to a LAN whose upstream connection provides a higher rate. For links with variable capacity, such as wireless links, the effective rate is the lower of the signaled rate and the current link capacity.¶

The signaled rates do not account for other bottlenecks along the path to a remote peer. Clients MUST NOT use the signaled rates as a replacement for congestion control or other adaptive rate mechanisms, and MUST NOT use them to increase the sending rate of a congestion control algorithm, such as its initial sending rate, beyond the rate that the algorithm would otherwise use. Where rate limits need to be enforced, enforcement remains the responsibility of the network; the DHCP Rate Option only informs clients about such limits.¶

8. Operational Considerations

Deploying explicit rate signaling via DHCP introduces several operational benefits and deployment considerations for network management. When combined with appropriately configured AQM and, where deployed, L4S-compatible queue management, these per-subscriber rate parameters help to concentrate congestion control at a well-defined bottleneck and minimize queuing delay in the access segment.¶

Operators should signal the rate at the layer at which the enforcement point accounts traffic, together with the per-packet overhead of the enforcement point whenever it is known (see Section 3.2.5). This allows clients to model the actual bottleneck precisely, including additional encapsulations and cell-based link layers. Where contracted rates are specified as payload rates, for example due to regulatory requirements, operators typically configure the enforcement point such that the payload rate achieved with maximum-sized packets meets the contracted rate; the signaled rate and overhead then describe the configuration of the enforcement point, not the contracted payload rate. The contracted rate can be conveyed in addition using the Advertised Rate sub-options (see Section 3.2.1).¶

Very-high-bit-rate Digital Subscriber Line 2 (VDSL2) commonly uses Packet Transfer Mode (PTM) with 64/65-octet encapsulation instead of ATM cells. Its effect corresponds to a constant reduction of the available rate and can be included in the signaled rate, without requiring cell-based accounting. Per-packet overhead of PTM, such as the Ethernet FCS, can be signaled using the Overhead field (see Section 3.2.5).¶

Where intermediate nodes enforce the signaled rates, operators should ensure that the enforcement at the intermediate node is not triggered by a client that shapes its traffic to the signaled rate, for example by applying a small correction factor at the intermediate node or by signaling node-specific rates (see Section 3.4 and Section 5.3). As enforcement points at intermediate nodes commonly account traffic at Layer 2, servers typically signal the Applied Rate Layer explicitly when provisioning intermediate nodes.¶

Where the actual rate of the access line is known and lower than the provisioned rate, for example due to the synchronization rate of a DSL line, operators should signal the lower of the two rates, so that the client shapes its traffic to the actual bottleneck. Appendix C describes how the parameters of the DHCP Rate Option can be derived from the access loop characteristics reported by the access node.¶

Without the use of DHCPFORCERENEW or Reconfigure, changes of the provisioned rates take effect with the next renewal of the client. Operators should consider this delay when choosing lease and renewal times, for example when rates are changed due to service upgrades or fair-use policies.¶

The inclusion of OPTION_RATE in the Parameter Request List or Option Request option allows operators to determine the share of clients supporting this option, for example to plan the introduction of correction factors or to identify clients that require enforcement at the network.¶

Implementations are encouraged to expose the signaled, DHCP-learned rate parameters, including Advertised Rates, within CPE management interfaces, such as the local Web User Interface (UI) or remote management protocols. Providing end-users and operators with immediate visibility into the locally provisioned service tier can reduce support inquiries related to perceived bandwidth issues.¶

As noted in Section 3.4, a client can receive an OPTION_RATE indicating an Applied Rate that exceeds the maximum physical link speed of its interfaces. In such scenarios, management interfaces should expose both the originally signaled rate value and the effective, capped rate value applied by the device. Additionally, implementations should log this discrepancy if logging facilities are enabled. Capturing and exposing these specific events provides useful information to network operators, as they can indicate a mismatch between the subscriber's provisioned service tier and their installed physical equipment.¶

9.1. Implementation Feasibility on Open-Source CPE

The authors analyzed the feasibility of implementing the client behavior on OpenWrt without modifying the DHCP client software. The DHCPv4 client (BusyBox udhcpc) and DHCPv6 client (odhcp6c) used by OpenWrt can request OPTION_RATE, send hints, and expose received options of unknown type as hexadecimal environment variables to user-defined hook scripts. All client behavior specified in this document can therefore be implemented in such scripts, which parse the option, apply the processing rules of Section 3.4, and configure local traffic management (e.g., via Smart Queue Management (SQM) or the Common Applications Kept Enhanced (CAKE) queue discipline). The parameters of CAKE can be derived from the Per-Packet Overhead sub-options (see Section 3.2.5): for a Layer 3 rate, the Overhead corresponds directly to the overhead parameter of CAKE, which is relative to the network-layer packet, while for a Layer 2 rate, the length of the Layer 2 headers on the link is added. The MPU corresponds to the mpu parameter of CAKE, and cell-based accounting with a Cell Payload of 48 and a Cell Size of 53 corresponds to its atm parameter. The rates learned on the WAN interface can be propagated to LAN clients using the existing custom-option support of the OpenWrt DHCPv4 server (dnsmasq) and DHCPv6 server (odhcpd).¶

10. Security Considerations

DHCP messages are typically transmitted as plaintext and are unauthenticated. Consequently, the DHCP Rate Options defined in this document are vulnerable to interception, modification, or spoofing by on-path attackers. This applies to any path the option traverses, including the path between servers and clients as well as the path between servers and relay agents, where an OPTION_RATE carried in a Relay-reply message configures the relay agent itself (see Section 5.4). A malicious actor or a rogue DHCP server could inject artificially low rate limits to throttle a client's connection, resulting in a localized Denial of Service (DoS).¶

The severity of this specific risk is generally no greater than standard DHCP threat vectors, such as rogue default gateway assignment, DNS hijacking, or IP pool exhaustion, which typically yield a much more critical and immediate loss of service.¶

Operators can mitigate rogue DHCP servers by deploying DHCP snooping for DHCPv4 and DHCPv6-Shield [RFC7610] for DHCPv6 on access nodes, which discard server messages received on client-facing ports. These mechanisms protect both the clients and the intermediate nodes that apply signaled rates.¶

To mitigate the impact of malicious or malformed options, client implementations MAY apply sanity checks before applying signaled rates. In particular, a client implementation MAY assume a minimum rate below which basic connectivity is no longer ensured, and treat an OPTION_RATE signaling a non-zero rate below this minimum as if it were not present (see Section 3.4). The appropriate minimum depends on the deployment and the use case of the device and is therefore left to the client implementation. For example, a broadband CPE might reject rates below 1 Mbit/s.¶

Excessive values of the Overhead or MPU fields of the Per-Packet Overhead sub-options reduce the effective rate. Receivers MAY limit these values to a plausible range or ignore the sub-option, which does not invalidate the OPTION_RATE (see Section 3.2.5). Manipulated Advertised Rate sub-options only affect informational uses, such as the display of rates or estimates used by applications.¶

Conversely, an attacker could inject an excessively high rate, a rate value of 0, or an OPTION_RATE without Applied Rate sub-options, thereby removing any effective rate limit (see Section 3.4). Because clients are expected to cap the effective rate to the capacity of the physical link (see Section 3.4), the impact is limited to the loss of the benefits of rate signaling, such as increased queuing delay at the bottleneck, which corresponds to the behavior of a device without support for this option. Where an intermediate node derives the enforcement of the provisioned rate from the DHCP Rate Option (see Section 1.2), however, an attacker on the path between the server and that node that raises or removes the signaled rates also raises or removes the enforcement, allowing a client to exceed its provisioned rate. Mechanisms for securing the messages exchanged between servers and relay agents are described in [RFC8213].¶

In local area networks, DHCP snooping is often not deployed, making rogue DHCP servers more likely. A rogue server signaling artificially low rates could cause applications on end devices to reduce their quality or to prefer another network interface, which can incur additional cost (e.g., cellular data). Clients SHOULD NOT use the signaled rates as the sole criterion for selecting a network interface, and the sanity checks described above apply.¶

Relay agents and snooping devices that instantiate traffic management functions based on the DHCP Rate Option consume finite resources, such as hardware queues and shapers. Because only server messages received on trusted ports are processed and rates are applied per client-facing port (see Section 3.4), the amount of such resources is bounded by the number of client-facing ports. However, a client can trigger frequent DHCP exchanges, potentially causing repeated reconfiguration of these functions. In addition to not reconfiguring unchanged rates (see Section 3.4), implementations MAY rate-limit DHCP messages received on client-facing ports or the reconfiguration of traffic management functions.¶

Hints are unauthenticated input from the client. Servers ought not to grant rates above the provisioned rate on the basis of hints, and relay agents and snooping devices do not use hints for their traffic management (see Section 3.6).¶

11. Privacy Considerations

The general privacy considerations for DHCP specified in [RFC7844] apply to this document.¶

The DHCP Rate Option conveys the rates provisioned for a client, which typically reflect the service tier of a subscriber. Because DHCP messages are typically transmitted as plaintext, this information is exposed to on-path observers, as well as to relay agents and snooping devices that process the option. The rate information itself does not identify a subscriber, but in combination with other information it can reveal commercial details of the service contract. This applies in particular to the Advertised Rate sub-options, which can convey the contracted rate directly; servers include them only where they are useful, for example not towards every client in a local area network.¶

Clients that include OPTION_RATE in the Parameter Request List or Option Request option, or that send hints (see Section 3.6), disclose additional information that can contribute to fingerprinting the device. Hints can additionally reveal hardware characteristics, such as the capacity of the client's physical interface. Clients that implement the anonymity profiles specified in [RFC7844] SHOULD NOT send hints and MAY omit OPTION_RATE from the Parameter Request List or Option Request option.¶

The operational practice of exposing and logging signaled and effective rates (see Section 8) can provide operators with information about the equipment installed by a subscriber. Such data is subject to the operator's applicable privacy policies.¶

Appendix A. Design Rationale

This appendix summarizes the reasoning behind the mechanism defined in this document and addresses questions that are frequently raised.¶

A.1. Why Is Rate Enforcement at the BNG Not Sufficient?

Rate enforcement at the BNG ensures that a subscriber does not exceed the provisioned rate, but it does not ensure that the subscriber can use this rate efficiently. In the upstream direction, enforcement is typically implemented with ingress policers that discard excess traffic without queuing it. Senders that are unaware of the policed rate experience bursty losses, which leads to unstable throughput below the provisioned rate (see Figure 1). Furthermore, upstream prioritization at the CPE, for example of DNS, VoIP, or selected clients and applications, is ineffective as long as packets are discarded at the BNG regardless of their priority.¶

Moreover, the upstream bottleneck is not always located at the BNG. Where it is located at an access device, such as an ONT or a DSL modem, or within the access network, neither the BNG nor the access device typically manages the resulting queue. Shaping at the CPE is then the only practical means to control upstream latency and to make prioritization effective.¶

Shaping at the CPE complements the enforcement at the BNG: the BNG continues to enforce the provisioned rate, for example with a small correction factor (see Section 3.4), while the CPE ensures that this enforcement is not triggered under normal operation. CPE that do not support this option continue to be subject to the enforcement at the BNG, so that the provisioned service tier is maintained in all cases.¶

A.2. Why Not Shape Upstream Traffic at the BNG?

Shaping requires queuing. Some forwarding hardware used for BNGs does not support per-subscriber queuing in the upstream direction at all. On forwarding hardware based on virtual output queues, for example, queues are associated with a destination port and traffic class rather than with the subscriber from which the traffic is received. Where per-subscriber upstream queuing is supported, it is commonly restricted: depending on the platform, it is available only on specific line cards or ports, requires the forwarding hardware to operate in a dedicated mode, or consumes the same queuing, buffer, and scheduling resources that are required for downstream hierarchical QoS, thereby reducing the overall subscriber scale. Alternatives such as the recirculation of upstream traffic through the egress pipeline reduce the forwarding capacity. For these reasons, upstream enforcement is commonly implemented using policers, even on platforms that support ingress queuing.¶

Furthermore, even where upstream shaping at the BNG is available, the BNG has limited knowledge of the traffic it shapes. Traffic from multiple devices behind the CPE typically shares a single address due to Network Address Translation (NAT), so that prioritization of individual clients or applications in the home network can only be performed at the CPE.¶

Shaping at the CPE, in contrast, scales naturally: each CPE shapes only its own traffic using a single shaper, and the required resources are distributed across all subscribers. In addition, the queue is located at the device that has the most information about its traffic, allowing prioritization, flow queuing, and AQM per subscriber without any additional resources in the network.¶

A.3. Why Not Configure the Rate Manually or via TR-069?

The provisioned rate is typically unknown to the user, may change over time (e.g., due to service upgrades or fair-use policies), and differs from the physical link speed of the CPE. Manual configuration is therefore error-prone and rarely performed. Auto-configuration protocols such as TR-069 [TR069] are not deployed by all operators and are typically not available for customer-owned CPE. In addition, they only target the CPE and cannot provision intermediate nodes.¶

A.4. Why DHCP?

DHCP is already used by virtually all broadband CPE, is processed by the BNG acting as relay agent or server, and is visible to intermediate access nodes. Rates conveyed via DHCP are refreshed with every lease renewal and can be updated dynamically. The option is simple enough to be processed by event-driven scripts in existing DHCP clients, without changes to the DHCP client software (see Section 3.4).¶

A.5. Relation to Access Line Information

Standardized mechanisms exist to convey the characteristics of an access line from the access node to the network: access loop characteristics are carried in DHCP relay agent options and PPPoE tags [TR101], mapped to RADIUS attributes such as Actual-Data-Rate-Upstream and Actual-Data-Rate-Downstream [RFC4679], and reported via ANCP [RFC6320]. These mechanisms inform the BNG and the AAA infrastructure about what the access line can carry. No standardized mechanism, however, conveys the resulting rates to the CPE and to intermediate nodes. This document closes this gap, using the access line information as input for the signaled rates (see Appendix C).¶

A.6. Relation to Existing Proprietary Solutions

Several operators have successfully deployed proprietary mechanisms to provide the CPE with its provisioned rate, for example by embedding rate information in PPP authentication messages or by vendor-specific management interfaces. The deployment of such mechanisms demonstrates the need for this information at the CPE, and CPE implementations already provide the corresponding traffic management functions, including shaping at Layer 2 or at Layer 3. However, these mechanisms have significant limitations: they are typically supported only by operator-provided CPE, so that customer-owned CPE fall back to the policing behavior described above; rate changes often take effect only after the session is re-established; and the information is not available to intermediate nodes. This document defines a standardized and interoperable alternative that addresses these limitations.¶

PPPoA NULL 2 (Layer 2) 8 48 / 53
IPoA LLC 3 (Layer 3) 16 48 / 53
IPoA NULL 3 (Layer 3) 8 48 / 53

The Overhead values include the encapsulation headers within the AAL5 frame, such as the LLC and SNAP headers [RFC2684] and the padding of bridged frames, and the 8-octet AAL5 trailer. For an Ethernet data link, no cell-based accounting applies, and the Overhead accounts for each VLAN tag that is present at the enforcement point but not on the link of the receiver (4 octets per tag), as indicated by the VLAN tagging reported in the Access-Loop-Encapsulation TLV, as well as for any per-packet overhead of PTM, if known.¶
