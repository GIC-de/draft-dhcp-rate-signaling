DHCPv6 Options for Explicit Rate Signaling

Abstract

This document defines two options for the Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol for IPv6 (DHCPv6) that signal the intended upstream and downstream rates of a network connection to clients. Each rate applies to the combined IPv4 and IPv6 traffic of the corresponding logical interface. In broadband access networks, the Customer Premises Equipment (CPE) typically sends at the speed of its physical link while the upstream rate is enforced by policers at the service edge. By learning the upstream rate, the CPE can shape its traffic and manage the queue using prioritization and active queue management (AQM).¶

1. Introduction

1.1. Problem Statement

In broadband access networks, the rate available to a subscriber is typically lower than the speed of the physical link that connects the Customer Premises Equipment (CPE) to the network. The service edge is typically a Broadband Network Gateway (BNG) [TR101]. The available rate can also be limited by an access device connected to the CPE, such as an Optical Network Terminal (ONT) [G.984.1], a Digital Subscriber Line (DSL) modem, or a cable modem, or within the access network, for example by the synchronization rate of a DSL line. The CPE is typically unaware of these rates and sends at the speed of its physical link.¶

Per-subscriber queuing in the upstream direction is not supported by all BNG platforms. Where it is supported, it is often restricted to specific hardware or available only at reduced scale, as it competes for the same queuing and scheduling resources as downstream hierarchical scheduling. Upstream enforcement is therefore commonly implemented using ingress policers, which discard packets that exceed the configured rate and burst size instead of queuing them. As the speed of the physical link of the CPE can exceed the provisioned rate by an order of magnitude, traffic bursts are discarded at the policer. Congestion control algorithms react to these losses by repeatedly reducing their sending rate, resulting in an unstable sawtooth throughput pattern well below the provisioned rate. Users experience this as poor and fluctuating results, for example in speed tests, even though no part of the network is congested.¶

Where the upstream bottleneck is located at the access device or within the access network, the resulting queue is typically managed by neither active queue management (AQM) [RFC7567] nor prioritization. Depending on the buffering of the access device, this results in packet loss or in increased latency under load, affecting all of the subscriber's traffic.¶

In both cases, upstream prioritization at the CPE, for example of DNS, Voice over IP (VoIP), or video conferencing traffic, or of TCP acknowledgments, is ineffective. As long as the CPE sends at the speed of its physical link, its queues remain empty, and packets are discarded or delayed at the actual bottleneck regardless of their priority.¶

Because the downstream rate is typically enforced at the BNG using egress shapers, it is of lower relevance for traffic management at the CPE. It remains relevant for informational purposes, such as displaying the provisioned service tier in the CPE user interface.¶

1.2. Solution Overview

This document defines two DHCPv6 [RFC9915] options: the Upstream Rate option and the Downstream Rate option. Each carries one unsigned 64-bit integer: the intended rate for its direction in bits per second (bit/s). Each rate counts the IP header and its payload at the IP layer of the link to which the option applies (see Section 3.2) and covers the combined IPv4 and IPv6 traffic of that link (see Section 3.3). This document specifies the encoding, transport, and meaning of the options. The usage examples are illustrative; clients choose whether and how to use the information according to local policy (see Section 3.4).¶

A CPE that knows the upstream rate can use it to configure an upstream shaper. Queuing at the CPE can then be managed using prioritization, AQM, and flow queuing [RFC8290]. Where the queue at the CPE implements an AQM that supports the Low Latency, Low Loss, and Scalable Throughput (L4S) architecture [RFC9330], such as the DualQ Coupled AQM [RFC9332], it can additionally provide low latency for L4S traffic, provided that the shaper at the CPE is the bottleneck. When the shaper accounts for the bottleneck rate and burst tolerance, this can reduce policer drops and queuing in the access network (see Figure 1).¶

Enforcement at the BNG remains in place. The rate of the BNG policer can be configured slightly above the signaled rate to accommodate CPE shaping and burst behavior.¶

1.3. Applicability

The mechanism defined in this document is primarily designed for broadband access networks using IP over Ethernet (IPoE) [TR101] or Point-to-Point Protocol over Ethernet (PPPoE) [RFC2516]. In both cases, DHCPv6 is commonly used by the CPE to obtain addresses and delegated prefixes; in PPPoE deployments, it runs over the Point-to-Point Protocol (PPP) [RFC1661] session (see Section 7).¶

The options are defined for DHCPv6 only. DHCPv6 is available in dual-stack and IPv6-only deployments, including those that provide IPv4 service over IPv6, and a single protocol avoids reconciling rates signaled by two protocols for the same traffic. The rates nevertheless cover IPv4 traffic (see Section 3.3); DHCPv6 options that configure IPv4 service have precedent, for example the Address Family Transition Router (AFTR) Name option for DS-Lite [RFC6334] and the options for Mapping of Address and Port (MAP) [RFC7598]. Networks that do not use DHCPv6 on the link are outside the scope of this document.¶

DHCPv6 is widely deployed on CPE, including customer-owned devices and devices running open-source firmware. It provides an existing path for initial configuration and updates, complementing manual configuration and management protocols such as TR-069 [TR069].¶

The options are applicable to any access link on which DHCPv6 is used to configure the network-facing interface and on which the intended rates are determined by a subscription, contract, or policy, including enterprise access links. They convey provisioned, slowly changing values and do not describe the instantaneous capacity of links whose capacity varies over time, such as wireless or cellular links; on such links, they can serve as an upper bound. Signaling rates to relay agents or other network nodes, and to end devices in local area networks, is outside the scope of this document.¶

2. Conventions and Definitions

The key words "MUST", "MUST NOT", "REQUIRED", "SHALL", "SHALL NOT", "SHOULD", "SHOULD NOT", "RECOMMENDED", "NOT RECOMMENDED", "MAY", and "OPTIONAL" in this document are to be interpreted as described in BCP 14 [RFC2119] [RFC8174] when, and only when, they appear in all capitals, as shown here.¶

This document makes use of the terms defined in [RFC9915]. The following additional terms are used:¶

Downstream

The direction of traffic from the network toward the client, received by the client over the link on which the Rate Option was received.¶

Enforcement Point

The node or link at which the rates of a subscriber are enforced or which constitutes the bottleneck of the path, for example a policer at the BNG or an access link.¶

Intended Rate

The maximum rate, in a given direction, at which the network intends to forward the traffic of a client. Traffic exceeding the intended rate is subject to being dropped or delayed at the enforcement point. The intended rate is not a guarantee: the achievable rate can be lower, for example due to congestion elsewhere in the network. The Rate Options signal the intended rates; this document also refers to them as the signaled rates.¶

Layer 3 Rate

A rate calculated over the IP header and its payload at the IP layer of the link to which the Rate Option applies (see Section 3.2).¶

Rate Option

The Upstream Rate option (OPTION_UPSTREAM_RATE) or the Downstream Rate option (OPTION_DOWNSTREAM_RATE) defined in this document. Where the distinction is not relevant, this document refers to either as a Rate Option.¶

Subscriber

The customer of a network access service. Within the network, a subscriber is typically represented by a subscriber session at the BNG, for example an IPoE or PPPoE session, to which the subscribed rates and other per-subscriber policies are applied.¶

Upstream

The direction of traffic from the client toward the network, sent by the client over the link on which the Rate Option was received.¶

3. Rate Semantics

3.1. Rate Values

Each Rate Option carries a single rate as an unsigned 64-bit integer in network byte order (big-endian), expressing the rate in bits per second (bit/s), not in bytes (octets) per second. Throughout this document, Mbit/s and Gbit/s denote 10^6 and 10^9 bits per second, respectively. The largest representable rate is 2^64 - 1 bit/s (18,446,744,073,709,551,615 bit/s), approximately 18.45 Ebit/s, where Ebit/s denotes 10^18 bits per second.¶

The value 0 is not a valid rate. A server MUST NOT send a Rate Option with the value 0. A direction for which no rate is signaled is indicated by the absence of the respective option.¶

The two options are independent: a server can send either or both, and the presence or absence of one does not affect the processing of the other.¶

3.2. Layer 3 Rates

Servers and clients MUST interpret each rate as a Layer 3 rate: the number of bits per second counted over the IP header and its entire payload, including IP options, IPv6 extension headers, and transport headers. The reference is the IP layer of the physical or logical link on which the DHCPv6 exchange takes place.¶

This reference remains the same when the link is carried over a tunnel. If DHCPv6 runs inside a tunnel, the rate refers to the IP packets on that logical link, excluding the tunnel's carrier headers. If the link instead carries tunneled data, such as IPv4-in-IPv6 traffic from a Dual-Stack Lite (DS-Lite) basic bridging broadband (B4) element [RFC6333], the IPv6 header and its encapsulated payload are counted. Thus the reference is the IP layer of the DHCPv6-configured link, not necessarily the outermost IP header on the underlying physical interface. Translation is accounted for as seen on the reference link.¶

Layer 3 accounting excludes link-layer headers, trailers, padding, and physical-layer overhead. Virtual LAN (VLAN) tags or PPPoE and PPP headers added or removed along the path do not change the rate. IP encapsulation or translation between the client and the enforcement point can change the counted packet size, which operators need to take into account when selecting the signaled rate.¶

For 1500-octet TCP/IP packets without options or extension headers, TCP payload throughput is approximately 2% to 4% lower than the Layer 3 rate due to the IP and transport headers.¶

Traffic management that counts IP packet sizes can use the signaled rate directly. Traffic management that counts link-layer frames needs to exclude the counted link-layer overhead to reproduce Layer 3 accounting. This header compensation is local: only overhead actually counted by the implementation is removed, including padding where applicable. For example, a shaper counting an Ethernet header, one VLAN tag, PPPoE, and PPP, but no frame check sequence (FCS) or padding, subtracts 14 + 4 + 6 + 2 = 26 octets per packet. Overhead inserted later by hardware is not subtracted. Applying the same numeric rate without compensation yields a lower IP rate that depends on packet size; it does not change the meaning of the signaled value.¶

The Layer 3 meaning of the Rate Options is fixed. Future documents can define additional options, for example to signal link-layer overhead or rates at another layer; such options complement the Rate Options and do not change their meaning. A client that does not support such an option ignores it and uses the Rate Options as specified in this document.¶

3.3. IPv4 and IPv6 Traffic

Although the Rate Options are carried in DHCPv6, each rate describes the aggregate IP traffic of the service on the reference link in its direction, including both IPv4 and IPv6. The rates are not an allowance for IPv6 traffic only, and there is no separate allowance for IPv4: for example, a signaled upstream rate of 1 Gbit/s describes 1 Gbit/s in total for IPv4 and IPv6 together.¶

This applies regardless of how IPv4 is provided on the logical interface, for example natively and configured by DHCPv4 [RFC2131] or by the IP Control Protocol (IPCP) [RFC1332], or over IPv6 using a transition mechanism such as DS-Lite [RFC6333]. In the latter case, IPv4 traffic is counted as the encapsulating IPv6 packets on the reference link (see Section 3.2).¶

The rates cover only the traffic of the logical interface on which the DHCPv6 exchange takes place. Where IPv4 is provided on a different logical interface, for example in a separate PPPoE session or on a separate VLAN, the signaled rates do not apply to that IPv4 traffic.¶

3.4. Use of Signaled Rates

A client MAY use the signaled rates for traffic management, display, or other local purposes. This document does not require shaping, AQM, or a particular response to rates that exceed local link or processing capacity. These choices, including precedence over locally configured policies, are implementation and deployment decisions. For example, a CPE with a 1 Gbit/s connection to an external modem can display a provisioned rate of 2 Gbit/s together with the local link limit, whether or not it configures a shaper. A client that uses the rates for traffic management accounts for their Layer 3 meaning (Section 3.2) and for the traffic to which they apply (Section 3.3).¶

The rates are neither a guarantee of available capacity nor a replacement for congestion control. Clients MUST NOT use them to bypass congestion control or to increase their sending rates beyond what the congestion control algorithm would otherwise permit. Clients that make the rates available to applications MUST NOT present them as available capacity or as a sending rate; applications can use them only as an upper bound or for display. Enforcement of a service limit remains the responsibility of the network.¶

4. DHCPv6 Rate Options

4.1. Upstream Rate Option

The Upstream Rate option (OPTION_UPSTREAM_RATE) carries the intended Layer 3 rate for the upstream direction. It has the following format:¶

option-code

OPTION_UPSTREAM_RATE (TBD1).¶

option-len

8.¶

Upstream Rate

Unsigned 64-bit integer in network byte order, in bits per second, as specified in Section 3.¶

4.2. Downstream Rate Option

The Downstream Rate option (OPTION_DOWNSTREAM_RATE) carries the intended Layer 3 rate for the downstream direction. It has the following format:¶

option-code

OPTION_DOWNSTREAM_RATE (TBD2).¶

option-len

8.¶

Downstream Rate

Unsigned 64-bit integer in network byte order, in bits per second, as specified in Section 3.¶

4.3. Option Placement

The Rate Options are top-level options, not Identity Association (IA) sub-options. They can appear in Advertise messages, where they are informational (see Section 5), and in Reply messages sent in response to Solicit, Request, Renew, Rebind, and Information-request. A server MUST NOT include them in replies to Confirm, Release, or Decline, or in Reconfigure messages. A client MUST ignore a Rate Option in any other placement or exchange.¶

Clients MUST NOT send Rate Options, and servers MUST ignore Rate Options received from clients. This document does not define the use of Rate Options in the options field of Relay-forward or Relay-reply messages.¶

Both options are singleton options (Section 21 of [RFC9915]). A server MUST NOT include more than one instance of the same Rate Option in a message. [RFC9915] treats multiple instances of an option as separate instances; for the Rate Options, this document additionally specifies that a client that receives more than one instance of the same Rate Option MUST discard all instances of that option and treat it as absent.¶

A Rate Option whose option-len is not 8, or whose value is 0, is invalid. A client MUST discard an invalid Rate Option and treat it as absent. Invalid Rate Options do not invalidate otherwise valid DHCPv6 configuration.¶

5. Client Behavior

DHCPv6 clients that support the Rate Options SHOULD include both option codes in the Option Request option (ORO) of every Solicit, Request, Renew, Rebind, and Information-request message. This requests rate information and indicates support for receiving it; it does not indicate whether the client will use the rates for traffic management.¶

A client MUST NOT apply rates received in any message other than Reply. Rates in an Advertise message are informational and indicate the rates the server intends to supply; see Section 9 regarding their use in server selection.¶

The Rate Options are configuration information received from the server and are processed like other non-IA configuration options of [RFC9915]:¶

  • A valid Rate Option in a Reply to Solicit (with Rapid Commit), Request, Renew, Rebind, or Information-request replaces the previously received rate for its direction, regardless of which of these exchanges supplied the previous rate.¶

  • If a Reply to Renew, Rebind, or Information-request does not include a requested Rate Option that the client previously received, the client SHOULD stop using the rate for that direction, as specified in Section 18.2.10.5 of [RFC9915]. This applies regardless of which exchange supplied the rate; a client does not need to track the source of a rate.¶

  • The rates are part of the client's DHCPv6 configuration and are not associated with individual bindings. The client discards them when it discards that configuration, for example because all addresses and delegated prefixes have become invalid, because it switches to another server, or because the underlying link or PPP session terminates. Clients that use only Information-request refresh them as specified in Section 21.23 of [RFC9915].¶

Replies to Confirm, Release, or Decline, and Replies that contain a top-level Status Code option (Section 21.13 of [RFC9915]) with a value other than Success, do not change the rates; the effect of binding changes is covered by the rules above. A client MUST NOT treat a rate that it has stopped using as current DHCPv6-provided information; subsequent use of local defaults remains a local decision.¶

6. Server Behavior

When a DHCPv6 server that supports the Rate Options receives a Solicit, Request, Renew, Rebind, or Information-request message whose ORO includes the code of a Rate Option, and a rate is configured for the client for the respective direction, the server MUST include that Rate Option in the resulting Advertise and Reply messages. If no rate is configured for a direction, the server omits the respective option; for a client that previously received it, this withdraws the rate as specified in Section 5. Where several servers can respond to a client, for example after a Rebind, operators need to configure the rates consistently on all of them; otherwise, a Reply from a server without that configuration withdraws the rates.¶

The server MAY derive the rates from local configuration profiles, from Authentication, Authorization, and Accounting (AAA) systems such as Remote Authentication Dial-In User Service (RADIUS) [RFC2865], or from external policy servers.¶

Where the rates are provisioned via RADIUS, they can be carried as Rate Options in the DHCPv6-Options attribute [RFC9445], in which both options are permitted (see Section 11). Where the BNG operates as a relay agent and a separate DHCPv6 server configures the client, the BNG can convey this attribute to the server in the RADIUS option [RFC7037]. As the omission of a requested Rate Option withdraws the rate (Section 5), such a relay agent SHOULD include the RADIUS option in every Relay-forward message relaying a Solicit, Request, Renew, Rebind, or Information-request from the client, unless the server retains the rates for the client. In all cases, the server includes the Rate Options in its responses as specified in this section.¶

A server MAY include the Rate Options in its responses even if the client did not request them via the ORO, according to its configuration. As revocation by omission applies only to requested options (Section 18.2.10.5 of [RFC9915]), a server cannot withdraw unrequested rates by omitting them; such rates are discarded only as part of the client's configuration (see Section 5). This is a further reason for clients to request the Rate Options.¶

To update the rates of a client before its next scheduled renewal, a server MAY send a Reconfigure message as specified in Section 18.3.11 of [RFC9915], causing the client to initiate a Renew, Rebind, or Information-request transaction. As specified in [RFC9915], a server can send Reconfigure messages only to clients that have included the Reconfigure Accept option in their messages, and Reconfigure messages are authenticated using the Reconfiguration Key Authentication Protocol (RKAP; see Section 20.4 of [RFC9915]). Updated rates are conveyed in the subsequent Reply.¶

For clients that do not support Reconfigure, updated rates take effect with the next Renew or Rebind transaction or, for stateless clients, with the next Information-request transaction. Operators can control the timing of such updates through the T1 value and the Information Refresh Time option (Section 21.23 of [RFC9915]). As support for Reconfigure is limited in deployed clients, operators cannot generally rely on it for updates before the next renewal or information refresh.¶

7. PPPoE Deployments

In PPPoE [RFC2516] architectures, the CPE typically employs DHCPv6 over the PPP link to obtain an IPv6 prefix via prefix delegation [RFC9915], while IPv4 is configured using IPCP [RFC1332]. The DHCPv6 exchange conveys the Rate Options in the same way as in IPoE deployments, and the signaled rates apply to the combined IPv4 and IPv6 traffic of the PPP session (see Section 3.3).¶

As the signaled rates are Layer 3 rates, a CPE that attaches its shaper to the PPP interface applies them directly, without accounting for the PPPoE, PPP, Ethernet, or VLAN headers.¶

If a client receives rate information from other sources, for example within PPP authentication messages, precedence is a matter of local policy. DHCPv6 can refresh the information during a session.¶

8. Operational Considerations

By default, a server does not send Rate Options; rates are signaled only when configured by the operator. Implementations are expected to allow operators to inspect the rates in effect, for example per interface at a client, and to log changes to them. This document does not define a YANG data model; the Rate Options can be added to the YANG data model for DHCPv6 configuration [RFC9243] in a separate document.¶

9. Security Considerations

The security considerations of DHCPv6 [RFC9915] apply. Without protection against modification and spoofing, an attacker can advertise false rates. A client that uses an artificially low rate for traffic management could throttle its own connection. A falsely high rate or a withdrawal could remove a locally derived limit and increase queuing or loss. As the absence of a Rate Option withdraws a rate, an on-path attacker that removes Rate Options from DHCPv6 messages achieves the same effect as a forged withdrawal. Even a client that only displays rates can be misled; applications using the values could reduce quality or choose a more costly network interface.¶

Local policy can restrict whether received rates affect traffic management and can validate values against deployment-specific bounds. The full unsigned 64-bit range is valid on the wire; decoding, arithmetic, and conversion to local APIs need to avoid overflow, truncation, and unit errors. Choosing not to apply a value does not change its Layer 3 meaning. Clients SHOULD NOT use signaled rates as the sole criterion for selecting a server or network interface.¶

Operators can use DHCPv6-Shield [RFC7610] to reject server messages arriving from client-facing ports. This does not protect against a compromised authorized server or an attacker elsewhere on the server-to-client path. A client accepts rate information only as part of a valid DHCPv6 exchange and uses the restrictions specified in Section 4.3. As the rates also govern IPv4 traffic, protecting only the IPv4 configuration protocol does not protect them.¶

The security considerations of [RFC9445] and [RFC7037] apply to rates provisioned via RADIUS. A server MUST ignore Rate Options conveyed in the RADIUS option unless they are received from a relay agent that it trusts, for example over a path protected as described in [RFC8213].¶

As the rates are signaled to clients, they do not by themselves enforce a service limit; a forged rate affects only the traffic management of the client that receives it.¶

10. Privacy Considerations

The privacy considerations for DHCPv6 specified in [RFC7824] apply to this document. Clients can additionally use the anonymity profiles specified in [RFC7844].¶

The Rate Options convey the rates provisioned for a client, which typically reflect the service tier of a subscriber. Because DHCPv6 messages are typically transmitted in cleartext, this information is exposed to on-path observers, including relay agents. The rate information itself does not identify a subscriber, but in combination with other information it can reveal commercial details of the service contract.¶

A.1. Applicability to OpenWrt

Once option codes are assigned, the Rate Options can be supported on OpenWrt [OPENWRT] without changes to its DHCPv6 client, using the following existing components:¶

Requesting the options:

The netifd DHCPv6 protocol handler accepts additional option codes in the reqopts list of the interface configuration and passes them to the DHCPv6 client odhcp6c, which includes them in the ORO of its requests.¶

Receiving the options:

odhcp6c passes options that it does not process itself to its status script as environment variables named OPTION_<code>, containing the hex-encoded option value. The status script (/lib/netifd/dhcpv6.script) invokes user scripts in /etc/odhcp6c.user.d/ on each state change, such as bound, updated, rebound, informed, unbound, and stopped. A variable that is absent in an update corresponds to the withdrawal of the rate, and the unbound and stopped events correspond to the discarding of the client's configuration (see Section 5).¶

Applying the rates:

A user script converts the rates from bit/s to the kbit/s used by the Smart Queue Management (SQM) package [SQM] and updates the upload and download rates of the SQM instance on the WAN interface, which applies them using CAKE or fq_codel. On a PPPoE WAN interface, the shaper typically counts IP packets, so the rates apply directly; on Ethernet-based WAN interfaces, the SQM overhead settings provide the header compensation described in Section 3.2.¶

Shell arithmetic on OpenWrt uses signed 64-bit integers, so a script-based implementation needs to handle values above 2^63 - 1, for example by treating them as exceeding any local limit. A native implementation could instead decode the options in odhcp6c and expose the rates as interface data via netifd, which also makes them available to other components, such as the web interface.¶

Appendix B. Design Rationale

This section is to be removed before publishing as an RFC.¶

This appendix explains major design decisions to the working group. Permanent rationale is included in the relevant sections of this document.¶

B.1. DHCPv6 Only

Earlier versions of this document defined the Rate Option for both DHCPv4 and DHCPv6. This version defines options for DHCPv6 only; the permanent rationale is given in Section 1.3.¶

  • DHCPv6 is available where the mechanism is needed. Broadband CPE commonly use DHCPv6 for prefix delegation in both IPoE and PPPoE deployments, so the options reach the target devices without a second protocol.¶

  • IPv4-only deployments of the new options are unlikely. Providers and CPE vendors that implement new DHCP options for current services are very likely to support IPv6 as well, so an IPv4-only variant would add specification and implementation effort for a small and shrinking set of deployments.¶

  • A single protocol removes considerable complexity. Signaling the same rates through DHCPv4 and DHCPv6 required rules for selecting between the two protocols, for keeping their values consistent, and for withdrawal and lifetime that differed between the protocols. These rules are no longer needed.¶

  • IPv4 traffic remains covered. The rates describe the service on the logical interface, not an IP version, and apply to the combined IPv4 and IPv6 traffic, whether IPv4 is provided natively, configured by DHCPv4 or IPCP, or carried over IPv6 (see Section 3.3).¶

  • The DHCPv6 option space allows a simpler format. DHCPv4 option codes are scarce, which motivated a single option carrying both directions. With DHCPv6, separate Upstream Rate and Downstream Rate options make each direction independent and allow withdrawal by omission instead of a reserved zero value.¶

  • Access networks that do not use DHCPv6, such as IPv4-only access, and signaling to end devices in local area networks are outside the scope of this document.¶

  • If the working group identifies a need, a DHCPv4 option can be defined in a separate document that reuses the semantics specified in Section 3.¶

The authors invite feedback from the working group on whether a DHCPv4 option is needed.¶

B.2. Overhead Signaling

The accounting reference of the signaled rates has been discussed at length. Proposals included signaling Layer 2 rates instead of Layer 3 rates, signaling the per-packet link-layer overhead together with the rates, and a selector indicating the layer at which a rate is counted. Earlier versions of this document included such a layer selector. This version defines a single Layer 3 reference (see Section 3.2) for the following reasons:¶

  • Layer 3 is the most common case. A CPE typically attaches its shaper to the IP interface, such as the PPP interface or the IPoE interface, where packet sizes are counted at the IP layer, so the signaled rate can be applied directly.¶

  • A Layer 3 rate is independent of the encapsulation along the path. VLAN tags and PPPoE and PPP headers are added and removed between the CPE and the enforcement point, so the link-layer overhead seen by the CPE generally differs from the overhead counted at the enforcement point. A Layer 2 rate would only be meaningful together with a precise definition of the link and encapsulation to which it refers.¶

  • Overhead is often not known to the server. The per-packet overhead counted at the bottleneck depends on the access technology, framing, padding, and implementation, which a DHCPv6 server frequently cannot determine. Signaling an inaccurate overhead would not improve on a conservative Layer 3 rate.¶

  • A single reference avoids ambiguity. With one fixed meaning, all clients interpret a value identically, and no negotiation or capability signaling is needed. Implementations that count link-layer frames apply local header compensation as described in Section 3.2, and operators account for overhead at the enforcement point, for example by allowing headroom or by signaling a conservative rate.¶

Layer 3 remains the default reference of the options defined in this document and is not changed by any extension. If the working group identifies a need, signaling of Layer 2 rates or of link-layer overhead, for example the per-packet overhead and framing of the bottleneck link, can be defined in a separate document as additional options that complement the Rate Options defined here.¶

The authors invite feedback from the working group on whether such an extension is needed.¶

B.3. Relay-Directed Rates

Intermediate versions of this document allowed the server to signal separate rates to relay agents in Relay-reply messages, so that, for example, a BNG or an access node operating as a Lightweight DHCPv6 Relay Agent (LDRA) could configure per-subscriber shapers and policers with values that differ from those signaled to the client. This mechanism has been removed from this document for the following reasons:¶

  • Server-to-relay configuration without a request by the relay agent is a new pattern for DHCPv6 and would require relay agents to maintain per-client state, contrary to the stateless relay agent model.¶

  • Withdrawal by omission and the loss of state after a relay agent restart would remove enforcement until the client's next exchange, and updates would depend on client renewal timing.¶

  • The server has no indication of whether a relay agent applied the rates, and the mapping of Relay-reply encapsulation levels to devices depends on the relay topology.¶

  • BNGs commonly obtain per-subscriber policies via AAA, which also supports updates during a session.¶

Signaling of rates to relay agents can be specified in a separate document if the working group identifies a need. The core mechanism defined here, rates signaled to clients, is independent of such an extension.¶

C.1. Since draft-ietf-intarea-dhcp-rate-signaling-00

  • Changed the intended status to Standards Track; DHCPv6 base reference is now RFC 9915.¶

  • Limited the scope to DHCPv6 and to rates signaled to clients in broadband access networks. Removed the DHCPv4 option, DHCPv6 snooping, rates signaled to relay agents, and the LAN, enterprise, and variable-capacity scenarios (see Appendix B).¶

  • Made the use of the rates a local decision and aligned client processing with RFC 9915, independent of the exchange that supplied a rate.¶

  • Added optional provisioning via RADIUS (RFC 9445, RFC 7037) and the corresponding IANA registration.¶

  • Condensed operational, security, privacy, and IANA considerations; removed the implementation example; added OpenWrt applicability and a design rationale appendix.¶
