DHCP Explicit Rate Signaling

Abstract

This document defines Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol (DHCP) options for DHCPv4 and DHCPv6 that signal the intended upstream and downstream rates of a network connection to clients and intermediate nodes. In many broadband access networks, the upstream rate is enforced by ingress policers at the service edge, while the Customer Premises Equipment (CPE) sends at the speed of its physical link, resulting in unstable throughput and ineffective prioritization. By learning the upstream rate via DHCP, the CPE can shape its traffic and move the queue to where it can be managed by prioritization and active queue management (AQM). Intermediate nodes can use the information to configure per-subscriber shapers and policers. All rates count IP headers and payloads, independently of link-layer encapsulation.¶

1. Introduction

1.1. Problem Statement

In broadband access networks, the rate available to a subscriber is typically lower than the speed of the physical link that connects the Customer Premises Equipment (CPE) to the network. The service edge is typically a Broadband Network Gateway (BNG) [TR101]. The available rate can also be limited by an access device connected to the CPE, such as an Optical Network Terminal (ONT) [G.984.1], a Digital Subscriber Line (DSL) modem, or a cable modem, or within the access network, for example by the synchronization rate of a DSL line. The CPE is typically unaware of these rates and sends at the speed of its physical link.¶

Per-subscriber queuing in the upstream direction is not supported by all BNG platforms. Where it is supported, it is often restricted to specific hardware or available only at reduced scale, as it competes for the same queuing and scheduling resources as downstream hierarchical scheduling. Upstream enforcement is therefore commonly implemented using ingress policers, which discard packets that exceed the configured rate and burst size instead of queuing them. As the speed of the physical link of the CPE can exceed the provisioned rate by an order of magnitude, traffic bursts are discarded at the policer. Congestion control algorithms react to these losses by repeatedly reducing their sending rate, resulting in an unstable sawtooth throughput pattern well below the provisioned rate. Users experience this as poor and fluctuating results, for example in speed tests, even though no part of the network is congested.¶

Where the upstream bottleneck is located at the access device or within the access network, the resulting queue is typically managed by neither active queue management (AQM) [RFC7567] nor prioritization. Depending on the buffering of the access device, this results in packet loss or in increased latency under load, affecting all of the subscriber's traffic.¶

In both cases, upstream prioritization at the CPE, for example of DNS, Voice over IP (VoIP), or video conferencing traffic, or of TCP acknowledgments, is ineffective. As long as the CPE sends at the speed of its physical link, its queues remain empty, and packets are discarded or delayed at the actual bottleneck regardless of their priority.¶

Because the downstream rate is typically enforced at the BNG using egress shapers, it is of lower relevance for traffic management at the CPE. It remains relevant for intermediate nodes that apply per-subscriber traffic management in both directions, and for informational purposes, such as displaying the provisioned service tier in the CPE user interface.¶

A similar lack of visibility exists within local area networks (LAN), such as home, enterprise, or public Wi-Fi hotspot networks, where end devices are typically connected at link speeds that exceed the capacity of the upstream connection (see Section 7.2).¶

1.2. Solution Overview

This document defines a Rate Option for versions 4 and 6 of the Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol (DHCPv4 and DHCPv6) that carries two values: the intended upstream and downstream rates, as unsigned 64-bit integers in bits per second (bps). Each rate counts the IP header and its payload at the IP layer of the link to which the option applies (see Section 3.1). This document specifies the encoding, transport, and meaning of the options. The usage examples are illustrative; receivers choose whether and how to use the information according to local policy (see Section 3.2).¶

A CPE that knows the upstream rate can use it to configure an upstream shaper. Queuing at the CPE can then be managed using prioritization, AQM, and flow queuing [RFC8290]. Where the queue at the CPE implements an AQM that supports the Low Latency, Low Loss, and Scalable Throughput (L4S) architecture [RFC9330], such as the DualQ Coupled AQM [RFC9332], it additionally provides low latency for L4S traffic. When the shaper accounts for the bottleneck rate and burst tolerance, this can reduce policer drops and queuing in the access network (see Figure 1).¶

Enforcement at the BNG remains in place. Its rate can be configured slightly above the signaled rate to accommodate CPE shaping and burst behavior (see Section 8).¶

Intermediate nodes can use the same information. In many IP over Ethernet (IPoE) [TR101] architectures, the BNG operates as a DHCP relay agent. Where per-subscriber policies are not provisioned via Authentication, Authorization, and Accounting (AAA) protocols such as Remote Authentication Dial-In User Service (RADIUS) [RFC2865], the BNG can use signaled rates to configure downstream shapers and upstream policers, and access nodes inspecting DHCP exchanges can use them to configure their queues and shapers. DHCPv6 additionally allows the server to signal different rates to the client and to individual relay agents in the path (see Section 5.3).¶

1.3. Applicability

The mechanism defined in this document is primarily designed for broadband access networks using IPoE or Point-to-Point Protocol over Ethernet (PPPoE) [RFC2516]. In PPPoE deployments, DHCPv6 is commonly used over the Point-to-Point Protocol (PPP) [RFC1661] session for IPv6 Prefix Delegation and conveys the options in the same way (see Section 7.1). The options are defined for both DHCPv4 and DHCPv6 to support IPv4-only, IPv6-only, and dual-stack deployments.¶

DHCP is widely deployed on CPE, including customer-owned devices and devices running open-source firmware, and its exchanges are visible to intermediate nodes along the path. It provides an existing path for initial configuration and updates, complementing manual configuration and management protocols such as TR-069 [TR069].¶

The options are applicable to any access link on which DHCP is used to configure the network-facing interface and on which the intended rates are determined by a subscription, contract, or policy, for example enterprise access links (see Section 7.3) and public or guest Wi-Fi networks (see Section 7.2). They convey provisioned, slowly changing values and do not describe the instantaneous capacity of links whose capacity varies over time (see Section 7.4).¶

2. Conventions and Definitions

The key words "MUST", "MUST NOT", "REQUIRED", "SHALL", "SHALL NOT", "SHOULD", "SHOULD NOT", "RECOMMENDED", "NOT RECOMMENDED", "MAY", and "OPTIONAL" in this document are to be interpreted as described in BCP 14 [RFC2119] [RFC8174] when, and only when, they appear in all capitals, as shown here.¶

This document makes use of the terms defined in [RFC2131] and [RFC9915]. The following additional terms are used:¶

Binding

The DHCP configuration state of a client for a given protocol. For a client, this is the DHCPv4 lease or the Identity Associations (IAs) obtained via DHCPv6. For an intermediate node, this is the state that it maintains for a client, associating the client with its client-facing port or session and with the addresses or prefixes leased to it, for example the subscriber session of a BNG or the client state maintained by a device inspecting DHCP exchanges.¶

DHCP

The abbreviation DHCP is used throughout this document to refer to both DHCPv4 and DHCPv6 protocols.¶

DHCPv4

The Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol [RFC2131].¶

DHCPv6

The Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol for IPv6 [RFC9915].¶

Downstream

The direction of traffic from the network towards the client. For a client, this corresponds to traffic received over the link on which the Rate Option was received. For an intermediate node, this corresponds to traffic sent on the client-facing interface or port.¶

Enforcement Point

The node or link at which the provisioned rates are enforced or which constitutes the bottleneck of the path, for example a policer at the BNG or an access link.¶

Intended Rate

The maximum rate, in a given direction, at which the network intends to forward the traffic of a client. Traffic exceeding the intended rate is subject to being dropped or delayed at the enforcement point. The intended rate is not a guarantee: the achievable rate can be lower, for example due to congestion elsewhere in the network. The Rate Option signals the intended rates; this document also refers to them as the signaled rates.¶

Intermediate Node

A device on the path between a DHCP client and server that processes the Rate Option for that client. This includes DHCPv4 and DHCPv6 relay agents, Layer 2 DHCPv4 Relay Agents [TR101], Lightweight DHCPv6 Relay Agents (LDRAs) [RFC6221], and devices that inspect DHCP exchanges without acting as relay agents (see Section 6).¶

Layer 3 Rate

A rate calculated over the IP header and its payload at the IP layer of the link to which the Rate Option applies (see Section 3.1).¶

Provisioned Rate

The rate configured in the network for a subscriber according to the subscribed service, typically enforced at the enforcement point (e.g., the BNG). The intended rates are typically derived from the provisioned rates.¶

Rate Option

The DHCPv4 Rate Option (OPTION_V4_RATE) or the DHCPv6 Rate Option (OPTION_V6_RATE) defined in this document. Where the distinction is not relevant, this document refers to both as the Rate Option.¶

Receiver

A DHCP client or intermediate node that processes a received Rate Option.¶

Subscriber

The customer of a network access service. Within the network, a subscriber is typically represented by a subscriber session at the BNG (e.g., an IPoE or PPPoE session), to which the provisioned rates and other per-subscriber policies are applied.¶

Upstream

The direction of traffic from the client towards the network. For a client, this corresponds to traffic sent over the link on which the Rate Option was received. For an intermediate node, this corresponds to traffic received on the client-facing interface or port.¶

3. Rate Option

This document defines a Rate Option for both DHCPv4 and DHCPv6. The option code and length follow the conventions of the respective base protocol (see Section 4 and Section 5). The option value has the same fixed 16-octet format in both protocols, and its semantics, specified in this section, are identical. A single fixed-format option keeps both directions together, uses one option code per protocol, and requires no sub-option parsing or registry.¶

The option value consists of two fields, in the following order:¶

Upstream Rate

The intended Layer 3 rate for the upstream direction.¶

Downstream Rate

The intended Layer 3 rate for the downstream direction.¶

Both fields are unsigned 64-bit integers in network byte order (big-endian), expressing the rate in bits per second (bps), not in bytes (octets) per second. Throughout this document, Mbit/s and Gbit/s denote 10^6 and 10^9 bits per second, respectively. The largest representable rate is 2^64 - 1 bps (18,446,744,073,709,551,615 bps), approximately 18.45 Ebit/s, where Ebit/s denotes 10^18 bits per second.¶

A value of 0 indicates that no rate is signaled for the respective direction. Its processing is specified in Section 3.3.¶

3.1. Layer 3 Rates

Senders and receivers MUST interpret each non-zero rate as a Layer 3 rate: the number of bits per second counted over the IP header and its entire payload, including IP options, IPv6 extension headers, and transport headers. For a client, the reference is the IP layer of the physical or logical link on which the DHCP exchange takes place. For an intermediate node, it is the IP layer of the corresponding client-facing link. A rate describes the aggregate IP traffic of the associated service in its direction, including both IPv4 and IPv6; the DHCP version carrying it does not restrict it to one IP version. In a LAN, the service can be the upstream connection described in Section 7.2.¶

This reference remains the same when the link is carried over a tunnel. If DHCP runs inside a tunnel, the rate refers to the IP packets on that logical link, excluding the tunnel's carrier headers. If the link instead carries tunneled data, such as IPv4-in-IPv6 traffic from a Dual-Stack Lite (DS-Lite) basic bridging broadband (B4) element [RFC6333], the IPv6 header and its encapsulated payload are counted. Thus the reference is the IP layer of the DHCP-configured link, not necessarily the outermost IP header on the underlying physical interface. Translation is accounted for as seen on the reference link.¶

Layer 3 accounting excludes link-layer headers, trailers, padding, and physical-layer overhead. Virtual LAN (VLAN) tags or PPPoE and PPP headers added or removed along the path do not change the rate. IP encapsulation or translation between the receiver and the enforcement point can change the counted packet size and requires consideration by the operator (see Section 8).¶

For 1500-octet TCP/IP packets without options or extension headers, TCP payload throughput is around 2 to 4% lower than the Layer 3 rate due to the IP and transport headers.¶

Traffic management that counts IP packet sizes can use the signaled rate directly. Traffic management that counts link-layer frames needs to exclude the counted link-layer overhead to reproduce Layer 3 accounting. This header compensation is local: only overhead actually counted by the implementation is removed, including padding where applicable. For example, a shaper counting an Ethernet header, one VLAN tag, PPPoE, and PPP, but no frame check sequence (FCS) or padding, subtracts 14 + 4 + 6 + 2 = 26 octets per packet. Overhead inserted later by hardware is not subtracted. Applying the same numeric rate without compensation yields a lower IP rate that depends on packet size; it does not change the meaning of the signaled value.¶

3.2. Use of Signaled Rates

A receiver MAY use the signaled rates for traffic management, display, or other local purposes. This document does not require shaping, policing, AQM, or a particular response to rates that exceed local link or processing capacity. These choices, including precedence over locally configured policies, are implementation and deployment decisions. For example, a CPE with a 1 Gbit/s connection to an external modem can display a provisioned rate of 2 Gbit/s together with the local link limit, whether or not it configures a shaper.¶

A receiver that uses the rates for traffic management accounts for their Layer 3 meaning (Section 3.1) and the traffic to which they apply. It selects one upstream/downstream pair for the service on the physical or logical interface. Each selected rate applies to the combined IPv4 and IPv6 traffic in its direction, regardless of which DHCP protocol supplied it. The two protocols do not provide separate or additive allowances: for example, a selected upstream rate of 1 Gbit/s describes 1 Gbit/s in total for IPv4 and IPv6 together. An intermediate node associates each rate pair with the corresponding client, rather than treating it as a limit for all clients on a shared port. In a LAN, a rate for an upstream connection does not describe the capacity for local traffic (see Section 7.2).¶

The rates are neither a guarantee of available capacity nor a replacement for congestion control. Receivers MUST NOT use them to bypass congestion control or to increase their sending rates beyond what the congestion control algorithm would otherwise permit. Enforcement of a service limit remains the responsibility of the network.¶

3.3. Rate Processing

3.3.1. Stored State and Source Selection

A receiver that retains rate information stores an upstream/downstream pair per link and DHCP protocol, together with the supplying server's identity and whether the pair was learned through lease assignment or an information-only exchange. Intermediate nodes maintain this state per client. Only configuration responses accepted under the applicable DHCP protocol and the message restrictions in Section 4 and Section 5 can update it.¶

For each DHCP protocol, if several servers supply configuration on a link, the receiver selects the source of rate information according to local policy from those accepted by that protocol. It accepts rate updates and withdrawals only from that source. When the selected source changes, the receiver MUST clear the previous pair before processing the new source's response; omission by the new source does not preserve information from the old source.¶

A valid Rate Option from the selected source replaces the pair and its lifetime association. DHCPv4 receivers obtain it from DHCPACK; DHCPv6 clients obtain it from Reply, and DHCPv6 relays obtain it from their own Relay-reply level carrying that Reply. A zero value withdraws the rate for that direction; two zero values clear the pair. Zero means neither a zero-bit-per-second service nor unlimited capacity.¶

3.3.2. Lifetime and Withdrawal

The exchange that supplied the pair determines its lifetime:¶

  • A pair learned through DHCPv4 lease assignment is associated with that lease and MUST be cleared when the lease expires, is released, or is otherwise invalidated.¶

  • A pair learned through a DHCPv6 Solicit/Reply, Request/Reply, Renew/Reply, or Rebind/Reply exchange is associated with all current bindings for non-temporary addresses (IA_NA) and delegated prefixes (IA_PD) supplied by that server to that client on the link, including bindings not repeated in the latest Reply. Renewals and additions update this association. The pair MUST be cleared when no valid address or delegated prefix remains in those bindings. Releasing one IA does not clear the pair while another remains valid; bindings from other servers do not extend its lifetime.¶

  • A pair learned through DHCPINFORM or DHCPv6 Information-request is associated with that server's information-only configuration on the link, independently of address or prefix bindings. It remains valid until replaced, withdrawn, or that configuration is discarded. DHCPv6 refresh follows Section 21.23 of [RFC9915]. A subsequent valid Rate Option replaces this association according to the exchange that supplies it, including transitions to or from lease assignment.¶

  • All pairs MUST be cleared when the underlying link or session terminates. For an intermediate node that does not track per-client bindings, the client-facing port or session supplies the lifetime instead; the node clears the corresponding pair when that port or session terminates.¶

For DHCPv6 client-directed information, omission of a previously received, requested Rate Option from an accepted Reply to Renew, Rebind, or Information-request is processed as specified in Section 18.2.10.5 of [RFC9915]: the client SHOULD clear its DHCPv6 pair. Replies to Confirm, Release, or Decline do not withdraw rates by omission; binding changes are handled separately as specified above. Snooping devices tracking these exchanges follow the same rule for the client-directed information. Other omissions leave the pair unchanged within its source and lifetime association. In DHCPv4 and for relay-directed DHCPv6 information, a server explicitly withdraws rates using zero values.¶

3.3.3. Selection between DHCPv4 and DHCPv6

For client-directed information, when both protocol pairs exist for the same link, the receiver SHOULD select the DHCPv6 pair unless local policy specifies otherwise. Selection is of a complete pair, including any zero value; rates from different pairs are not combined. Clearing the selected pair makes the remaining pair, if any, available. If no pair remains, or a direction in the selected pair is zero, no signaled rate is available for the affected direction. A withdrawn rate MUST NOT be treated as current DHCP-provided information. Subsequent use remains a local decision.¶

Operators MUST configure identical client-directed intended rates for the same client and link in both protocols, or signal them through only one protocol. Renewal timing can cause temporary differences. Relay-directed DHCPv6 rates are separate information and need not equal those sent to the client. A dual-protocol relay SHOULD prefer its own relay-directed DHCPv6 pair over rates learned from client-directed DHCPv4 messages when selecting rate information for itself. If that pair is absent or withdrawn, use of DHCPv4-derived information or local configuration is a matter of local policy. A client-directed DHCPv6 pair is not a substitute for a relay-directed pair (see Section 5.4).¶

The stored values retain their signaled meaning even if local policy chooses different traffic management parameters. Storing and clearing rate information does not require traffic management.¶

3.4. Error Handling

In DHCPv4, the sender encodes one logical 16-octet Rate Option value. This value MAY be split across multiple option instances according to [RFC3396]. A receiver MUST concatenate all instances as specified there before validating the resulting value. Multiple complete values yield more than 16 octets and are malformed.¶

A DHCPv6 sender MUST NOT include more than one instance of the Rate Option at the same encapsulation level. A receiver that receives more than one instance at a level MUST discard all instances at that level and treat the option as absent there.¶

A Rate Option whose value is not 16 octets long is malformed. A receiver MUST discard a malformed Rate Option and treat it as absent, following the protocol-specific rules in Section 3.3. In DHCPv4, this length check applies after concatenation. Invalid Rate Options do not invalidate otherwise valid DHCP configuration.¶

A Rate Option in a disallowed message, placement, or exchange MUST be ignored as specified in Section 4 and Section 5; it does not update or withdraw a stored pair.¶

4. DHCPv4

4.1. DHCPv4 Rate Option

The DHCPv4 Rate Option (OPTION_V4_RATE), shown as a single option instance, has the following format. Splitting and concatenation follow Section 3.4.¶

Code

TBD1.¶

Length

16 when encoded as a single instance. The total value length after concatenation is 16 octets.¶

Upstream Rate, Downstream Rate

Unsigned 64-bit integers in network byte order (big-endian), in bits per second (bps), as specified in Section 3.¶

The DHCPv4 Rate Option can appear in the following messages:¶

Table 1: Appearance of the DHCPv4 Rate Option
Message Use
DHCPOFFER Server selection (see Section 4.2)
DHCPACK Rate information (see Section 3.3)

4.2. DHCPv4 Client Behavior

DHCPv4 clients that support the Rate Option SHOULD include its code in the Parameter Request List (PRL) of DHCPDISCOVER, DHCPREQUEST, and DHCPINFORM messages. This requests rate information and indicates support for receiving it; it does not indicate whether the client will use it for traffic management.¶

A client MUST NOT apply rates received in any DHCPv4 message other than DHCPACK. A client MAY use the rates contained in a DHCPOFFER message as a criterion for selecting among multiple offers. Rates received in a DHCPACK message are processed as specified in Section 3.3.¶

4.3. DHCPv4 Server Behavior

When a DHCPv4 server that supports the Rate Option receives a client message whose PRL includes the code of the Rate Option, and a rate is configured for the client for at least one direction, the server MUST include the Rate Option in the resulting DHCPOFFER and DHCPACK messages, with a value of 0 for a direction for which no rate is configured. As an absent Rate Option leaves the stored pair unchanged within its source and lifetime association, a server removes previously signaled rates by including the Rate Option with a value of 0 for the respective directions (see Section 3.3).¶

The server MAY derive the rates from local configuration profiles, centralized AAA systems such as RADIUS, or external policy servers.¶

A server MAY include the Rate Option in its responses even if the client did not request it via the PRL, provided the operator has explicitly configured the server to do so, for example to provide rate information to intermediate nodes.¶

To update the rates of a client before its next scheduled renewal, a server MAY send a DHCPFORCERENEW message [RFC3203], causing the client to initiate a renewal transaction. DHCPFORCERENEW messages are subject to the authentication requirements of [RFC3203], which can be satisfied using the mechanism specified in [RFC6704]. For clients that do not support DHCPFORCERENEW, updated rates take effect with the next renewal, the timing of which is controlled by the renewal (T1) time value.¶

4.4. DHCPv4 Relay Agent Behavior

DHCPv4 relay agents, including Layer 2 DHCPv4 Relay Agents [TR101], MAY extract the Rate Option from DHCPACK messages traversing the network and use the rates to configure shapers and policers on their client-facing interfaces. Layer 2 DHCPv4 Relay Agents MUST process only a Rate Option contained in DHCPACK messages received on trusted, network-facing ports.¶

Apart from removing the Relay Agent Information option [RFC3046], a DHCPv4 relay agent does not change the options of a server message destined for the client [RFC2131]. Consequently, a relay agent does not insert, replace, modify, or remove a Rate Option in that message. A device that provides the client with rates derived from subscriber attributes (e.g., retrieved from an AAA server such as RADIUS), for example a BNG, acts as the DHCPv4 server for the client and includes these rates when constructing its responses, as specified in Section 4.3. Devices that rewrite the message destined for the client while acting as a relay agent are outside the scope of this document.¶

5. DHCPv6

5.1. DHCPv6 Rate Option

The DHCPv6 Rate Option (OPTION_V6_RATE) has the following format:¶

option-code

TBD2.¶

option-len

16.¶

Upstream Rate, Downstream Rate

Unsigned 64-bit integers in network byte order (big-endian), in bits per second (bps), as specified in Section 3.¶

The DHCPv6 Rate Option can appear in the following messages:¶

Table 2: Appearance of the DHCPv6 Rate Option
Message Use
Advertise Server selection (see Section 5.2)
Reply Rate information for the client (see Section 3.3)
Relay-reply Rate information for the relay agent (see Section 5.4)

In client-directed messages, the Rate Option is a top-level option, not an IA sub-option. It can appear in Advertise and in Reply messages sent in response to Solicit, Request, Renew, Rebind, and Information-request, subject to Section 5.3. It is not included in replies to Confirm, Release, or Decline, or in Reconfigure messages. Relay-reply placement is specified in Section 5.3. A receiver MUST ignore a Rate Option in any other placement or exchange.¶

5.2. DHCPv6 Client Behavior

DHCPv6 clients that support the Rate Option SHOULD include its code in the Option Request option (ORO) [RFC9915] of every Solicit, Request, Renew, Rebind, and Information-request message, to request rate information. As with the DHCPv4 PRL, this does not indicate whether the client will use the rates for traffic management.¶

A client MUST NOT apply rates received in any DHCPv6 message other than Reply. A client MAY use the rates contained in an Advertise message as a criterion for selecting among multiple Advertise messages. Rates received in a Reply message are processed as specified in Section 3.3.¶

5.3. DHCPv6 Server Behavior

When a DHCPv6 server that supports the Rate Option receives a Solicit, Request, Renew, Rebind, or Information-request message whose ORO includes the code of the Rate Option, and a rate is configured for the client for at least one direction, the server MUST include the Rate Option in the resulting Advertise and Reply messages, with a value of 0 for a direction for which no rate is configured. The server MAY explicitly withdraw a rate using zero. When neither direction is configured, omission of the requested option from a Reply to Renew, Rebind, or Information-request also withdraws the client-directed information as specified in Section 3.3.¶

A server MAY include the Rate Option in its responses even if the client did not request it via the ORO, provided the operator has explicitly configured the server to do so.¶

In relayed exchanges, the server MAY include a Rate Option in the options field of a Relay-reply message, alongside its Relay Message option, to supply rates for the corresponding relay agent. This is independent of inclusion in the encapsulated client message and does not require the client to request the option in its ORO. No relay request option is required. Operators can configure this delivery using their knowledge of the relay topology and subscriber associations. The Relay Agent Echo Request option [RFC4994] requests the return of relay-supplied options; it is not needed to deliver these server-selected rates.¶

The server MAY supply different rates at different Relay-reply encapsulation levels and in the client message. Each pair refers to the identified client's traffic at its recipient, not to all traffic on an aggregate relay link. A server includes a relay-directed Rate Option only when the encapsulated client message is a Reply carrying configuration in response to Solicit, Request, Renew, Rebind, or Information-request. It does not include a relay-directed Rate Option around Advertise, Reconfigure, or replies to Confirm, Release, or Decline.¶

Relay delivery and processing are optional. Relays that do not recognize the option ignore it. Relays provisioned by other means, such as AAA, can continue using that configuration.¶

To update the rates of a client before its next scheduled renewal, a server MAY send a Reconfigure message as specified in Section 18.3.11 of [RFC9915], causing the client to initiate a Renew, Rebind, or Information-request transaction. As specified in [RFC9915], a server can only send Reconfigure messages to clients that have included the Reconfigure Accept option in their messages, and Reconfigure messages are authenticated using the Reconfiguration Key Authentication Protocol (RKAP; see Section 20.4 of [RFC9915]). Updated rates are conveyed in the subsequent Reply exchange.¶

For clients that do not support Reconfigure, updated rates take effect with the next Renew or Rebind transaction or, for stateless clients, with the next Information-request transaction. Operators can control the timing of such updates through the T1 value and the Information Refresh Time option (Section 21.23 of [RFC9915]).¶

5.4. DHCPv6 Relay Agent Behavior

DHCPv6 relay agents that support the Rate Option, including LDRAs [RFC6221], process a Rate Option contained in a Relay-reply message addressed to them as specified in Section 3.3. Such relay agents MAY use the signaled rates to configure shapers, policers, or AQM disciplines on their client-facing interfaces. Relay agents MUST process only Relay-reply messages received from the network side; in particular, an LDRA discards Relay-reply messages received on client-facing interfaces as specified in Section 6.1.1 of [RFC6221].¶

The relay uses only the Rate Option at its own Relay-reply encapsulation level as its relay-directed rate information. It MUST NOT substitute an option from another relay level or the client message. Absence of the option at its own level leaves its stored pair unchanged while the source and lifetime association remain valid (see Section 3.3). A server withdraws a relay-directed rate explicitly using zero, independently of the client's ORO. This preserves the server's ability to signal different rates to individual nodes.¶

A DHCPv6 relay agent does not change the message encapsulated in the Relay Message option (Section 19.2 of [RFC9915] and Section 19.4 of [RFC9915]). Consequently, a relay agent does not insert, replace, modify, or remove a Rate Option in the message destined for the client. A device that provides the client with rates derived from subscriber attributes, for example a BNG, acts as the DHCPv6 server for the client and includes these rates when constructing its responses, as specified in Section 5.3. Devices that rewrite the encapsulated message while acting as a relay agent are outside the scope of this document.¶

6. DHCP Snooping

Devices that inspect DHCP exchanges without acting as relay agents MAY obtain rate information from client-directed DHCPACK (DHCPv4) and Reply (DHCPv6) messages. They associate the information with the corresponding client and process it as described in Section 3.3, without modifying the DHCP messages.¶

As with the relay agents described in Section 4.4 and Section 5.4, including Layer 2 DHCPv4 Relay Agents and LDRAs, snooping devices MAY use the rates according to local policy. Traffic management is one possible use; receiving or inspecting the option does not require applying the rates. The examples in Section 3.2 also apply to these devices.¶

Snooping devices MUST process only Rate Options received from trusted network-facing interfaces or peers. Rate Options received from the client-facing side MUST be ignored. The security considerations in Section 9 apply, particularly when the information is used for service enforcement.¶

7. Deployment Scenarios

7.1. PPPoE

In PPPoE [RFC2516] architectures, the CPE typically employs DHCPv6 over the PPP link to obtain an IPv6 prefix via prefix delegation [RFC9915]. This DHCPv6 exchange conveys the Rate Option in the same way as in IPoE deployments. DHCPv4 transactions within a PPPoE session can similarly convey the DHCPv4 Rate Option.¶

The processing rules for a Rate Option received over PPPoE are identical to those defined for IPoE, as specified in Section 3.3. In particular, the stored rates are cleared when the PPPoE session is terminated.¶

As the signaled rates are Layer 3 rates, a CPE that attaches its shaper to the PPP interface applies them directly, without accounting for the PPPoE, PPP, Ethernet, or VLAN headers.¶

If a client receives rate information from other sources, for example within PPP authentication messages, precedence is a matter of local policy. DHCP can refresh the information during a session.¶

7.2. Local Area Networks

A DHCP server in a home, enterprise, or public Wi-Fi network can signal the rates of the upstream connection or a per-client service limit. These rates describe access to the upstream network, not the capacity for traffic between local devices. Unless a rate is enforced per client, it represents capacity shared by the LAN clients and does not guarantee a share to any one client.¶

Clients can display the rates, use them to estimate transfer times, or use them for traffic management according to local policy. For example, a Wi-Fi client might display its provisioned Internet rate separately from its wireless link speed. A client shaping Internet traffic needs to distinguish that traffic from local traffic. Congestion control remains necessary in all cases.¶

A CPE acting as a DHCP server on its LAN MAY derive the rates it signals from the Rate Option on its Wide Area Network (WAN) interface, local limits, or both. The derived values retain the Layer 3 meaning defined in Section 3.1. Updates or withdrawals can be conveyed at the next client exchange or triggered as described in Section 4.3 and Section 5.3. DHCP provides this information after network attachment; advertising capacity before Wi-Fi association is outside the scope of this document.¶

7.3. Enterprise Access Links

Enterprise branch offices are often connected via access links whose contracted rate is lower than the speed of the physical interface, for example a 100 Mbit/s service delivered over a 1 Gbit/s Ethernet interface, with the contracted rate enforced by a policer in the provider network. Where the provider uses DHCP to configure the customer edge router, the Rate Option allows the router, including Software-Defined Wide Area Network (SD-WAN) edge devices, to configure its upstream shaper without manual provisioning.¶

Where the service defines both a committed and an excess or peak rate, the intended rate is the maximum rate at which the provider intends to forward traffic (see Section 2). Bandwidth profiles of such services commonly measure rates using Ethernet frame sizes; the considerations for enforcement points that count traffic at Layer 2 apply (see Section 8).¶

7.4. Links with Variable Capacity

On wireless, cellular, and satellite access links, the available capacity varies with radio conditions, scheduling, and load on time scales far shorter than DHCP lease and renewal intervals. The Rate Option is not suitable for conveying such variations. It can, however, convey a provisioned upper bound, for example a rate limit defined by the subscription. Where the bottleneck is the variable-capacity link itself, a shaper configured with the signaled rate does not prevent queuing at that link; receivers can then use the signaled rate as an upper bound for mechanisms that adapt to the current capacity, such as shapers that adjust their rate based on measured delay or throughput.¶

8. Operational Considerations

Operators select the Layer 3 rates to signal according to the subscriber service and known access constraints. Access line information, such as the RADIUS Actual-Data-Rate-Upstream and Actual-Data-Rate-Downstream attributes [RFC4679] or information obtained through the Access Node Control Protocol (ANCP) [RFC6320], can inform that choice. Such values need conversion if their accounting differs from the IP-layer reference defined in Section 3.1.¶

Matching Layer 3 accounting at the receiver and enforcement point avoids differences caused by link-layer encapsulation. Rate matching alone does not ensure that a policer will accept every burst: burst tolerance, scheduling, and measurement differences also matter. Operators can allow headroom at the enforcement point or signal a lower rate to a client. DHCPv6 also permits separate rates for a relay and its client (see Section 5.3).¶

When an enforcement point counts link-layer overhead, or a physical access link is the bottleneck, the usable IP rate depends on packet sizes and encapsulation. A single fixed conversion factor cannot be exact for every traffic mix. Cell-based links introduce additional padding and framing costs. The Rate Option does not model these details; operators can use a conservative Layer 3 rate, and implementations can use locally configured link parameters when more precise shaping is needed. Header compensation at the receiver alone does not model a different bottleneck elsewhere in the network.¶

Rates are updated through DHCP exchanges. Operators account for renewal and information refresh timing when changing service tiers or policy, and can use the update mechanisms in Section 4.3 and Section 5.3 where supported. A client request for the Rate Option shows that it can receive rate information; it does not show whether it applies traffic management.¶

8.1. Example Implementation on Open-Source CPE

A DHCP client can expose rate information and configuration lifecycle events to hook scripts. For example, a script adds, updates, or deletes a file containing the rate pair for each DHCP protocol and logical interface, following Section 3.3, then calls a common evaluation function. That function selects the DHCPv6 pair when present, otherwise the DHCPv4 pair. It can display the result or use it for traffic management of the combined IPv4 and IPv6 traffic. If neither pair remains, it clears the displayed DHCP-derived rates or restores local traffic management defaults. These are implementation examples; using the rates for traffic management remains optional.¶

The client or script validates and decodes the option with integer handling that preserves all 64 bits. Lifecycle events need to reflect the source and lifetime rules in Section 3.3, including DHCPv6 withdrawal when a requested option is omitted. Releasing one of several valid DHCPv6 bindings from the same server does not delete the pair while another remains valid.¶

9. Security Considerations

The security considerations of DHCPv4 [RFC2131] and DHCPv6 [RFC9915] apply. Without protection against modification and spoofing, an attacker can advertise false rates. A receiver that uses an artificially low rate for traffic management could throttle its own connection. A falsely high rate or a withdrawal could remove a locally derived limit and increase queuing or loss. Even a client that only displays rates can be misled; applications using the values could reduce quality or choose a more costly network interface.¶

Local policy can restrict whether received rates affect traffic management and can validate values against deployment-specific bounds. The full unsigned 64-bit range is valid on the wire; decoding, arithmetic, and conversion to local APIs need to avoid overflow, truncation, and unit errors. Choosing not to apply a value does not change its Layer 3 meaning. Clients SHOULD NOT use signaled rates as the sole criterion for selecting a server or network interface.¶

Operators can use DHCP snooping and DHCPv6-Shield [RFC7610] to reject server messages arriving from client-facing ports. These measures do not protect against a compromised authorized server or an attacker elsewhere on the server-to-receiver path. A receiver accepts rate information only as part of a valid DHCP exchange and uses the source restrictions specified in Section 4, Section 5, and Section 6. Protecting only one DHCP protocol does not protect rate information received through the other protocol on a dual-stack link.¶

Where an intermediate node derives service enforcement from a Rate Option, forged rates can weaken that enforcement. Such a node SHOULD do so only when its path to the server is protected against modification, for example using IPsec as described in [RFC8213], or is within a trusted administrative domain inaccessible to subscribers. Otherwise, operators provision enforcement through other means, such as AAA. A client-side rate setting is not a substitute for network service enforcement.¶

Per-client rate state and traffic management consume finite resources. Implementations can bound that state and limit reconfiguration frequency; a shared port alone does not bound the number of client bindings. Avoiding reconfiguration for unchanged values also reduces work caused by frequent DHCP exchanges. Invalid Rate Options are handled as specified in Section 3.4 without invalidating otherwise valid DHCP configuration.¶

10. Privacy Considerations

The general privacy considerations for DHCP specified in [RFC7844] apply to this document.¶

The Rate Option conveys the rates provisioned for a client, which typically reflect the service tier of a subscriber. Because DHCP messages are typically transmitted as plaintext, this information is exposed to on-path observers, including intermediate nodes that process the options. The rate information itself does not identify a subscriber, but in combination with other information it can reveal commercial details of the service contract.¶

  • Changed the intended status to Standards Track and updated the DHCPv6 base reference to RFC 9915.¶

  • Replaced the sub-option format with two unsigned 64-bit values in bits per second, encoded in network byte order. Removed the sub-option registry, layer selector, and separate informational rates. These wire-format changes are not backward compatible with -00.¶

  • Defined a single Layer 3 interpretation at the IP layer of the DHCP-configured link, including the distinction between tunneled data and DHCP operating inside a tunnel. Combined header compensation with the Layer 3 definition and clarified the throughput estimate.¶

  • Separated client-directed dual-stack consistency and selection from relay-specific rates. Clarified that one selected pair covers combined IPv4 and IPv6 traffic, regardless of the delivering DHCP protocol, and illustrated lifecycle handling with CPE hook scripts.¶

  • Defined DHCPv6 Relay-reply placement and independent server-configured delivery without a client ORO or relay request. Clarified the separation of relay-directed and client-directed values.¶

  • Kept DHCPv4 and DHCPv6 relay behavior separate from passive snooping, with optional usage examples for both. Clarified that relay agents do not modify the client-directed option; retained source-validation and congestion-control requirements.¶
