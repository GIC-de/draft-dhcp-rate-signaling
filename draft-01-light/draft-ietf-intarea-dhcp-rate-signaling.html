DHCP Explicit Rate Signaling

Abstract

This document defines Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol (DHCP) options for DHCPv4 and DHCPv6 that signal the intended upstream and downstream rates of a network connection to clients and intermediate nodes. In many broadband access networks, the upstream rate is enforced by ingress policers at the service edge, while the Customer Premises Equipment (CPE) sends at the speed of its physical link, resulting in unstable throughput and ineffective prioritization. By learning the upstream rate via DHCP, the CPE can shape its traffic and move the queue to where it can be managed by prioritization and Active Queue Management (AQM). Relay agents and snooping devices can use the same information to configure per-subscriber shapers and policers. All rates are Layer 3 rates, which are independent of the link-layer encapsulation and closely match the speed perceived by users.¶

1. Introduction

1.1. Problem Statement

In broadband access networks, the rate available to a subscriber is typically lower than the speed of the physical link that connects the Customer Premises Equipment (CPE) to the network. The provisioned rate is commonly enforced at the Broadband Network Gateway (BNG) [TR101]. The upstream rate can additionally be limited by an access device connected to the CPE, such as an Optical Network Terminal (ONT) [G.984.1], a Digital Subscriber Line (DSL) modem, or a cable modem, or within the access network, for example by the synchronization rate of a DSL line. The CPE is typically unaware of these rates and sends at the speed of its physical link.¶

Per-subscriber queuing in the upstream direction is not supported by all BNG platforms. Where it is supported, it is often restricted to specific hardware or available only at reduced scale, as it competes for the same queuing and scheduling resources as downstream hierarchical QoS. Upstream enforcement is therefore commonly implemented using ingress policers, which discard packets that exceed the configured rate and burst size instead of queuing them. As the speed of the physical link of the CPE can exceed the provisioned rate by an order of magnitude, traffic bursts are discarded at the policer. Congestion control algorithms react to these losses by repeatedly reducing their sending rate, resulting in an unstable sawtooth throughput pattern well below the provisioned rate. Users experience this as poor and fluctuating results, for example in speed tests, even though no part of the network is congested.¶

Where the upstream bottleneck is located at the access device or within the access network, the resulting queue is typically managed by neither Active Queue Management (AQM) [RFC7567] nor prioritization. Depending on the buffering of the access device, this results in packet loss or in increased latency under load, affecting all traffic of the subscriber alike.¶

In both cases, upstream prioritization at the CPE, for example of DNS, Voice over IP (VoIP), or video conferencing traffic, or of TCP acknowledgments, is ineffective. As long as the CPE sends at the speed of its physical link, its queues remain empty, and packets are discarded or delayed at the actual bottleneck regardless of their priority.¶

In the downstream direction, the provisioned rate is typically enforced at the BNG using egress shapers. The downstream rate is therefore of lower relevance for traffic management at the CPE, but it is relevant for intermediate nodes that apply per-subscriber traffic management in both directions, and it is useful for informational purposes, for example to display the provisioned service tier in the user interface of the CPE.¶

A similar lack of visibility exists within local area networks (LANs), such as home, enterprise, or public Wi-Fi hotspot networks, where end devices are typically connected at link speeds that exceed the capacity of the upstream connection (see Section 7.2).¶

1.2. Solution Overview

This document defines a Rate Option for DHCPv4 and DHCPv6 that carries two values: the intended upstream rate and the intended downstream rate, as unsigned 64-bit integers in bits per second (bps). The rates are Layer 3 rates, i.e., they refer to the IP packets as transmitted on the link of the receiver (see Section 3.1).¶

A CPE that knows the upstream rate can shape its upstream traffic to this rate, regardless of where the bottleneck is located. The queue then builds at the CPE, where it can be managed using prioritization, AQM, and flow queuing [RFC8290]. Where the queue at the CPE implements an AQM that supports the Low Latency, Low Loss, and Scalable Throughput (L4S) architecture [RFC9330], such as the DualQ Coupled AQM [RFC9332], it additionally provides low latency for L4S traffic. The policer at the BNG is no longer triggered, and the access device is not congested (see Figure 1). The resulting throughput is stable and close to the signaled rate, and latency under load remains low.¶

The enforcement at the BNG remains in place for CPE that do not support the Rate Option; it can be configured slightly above the signaled rate so that it is not triggered by CPE that shape their traffic to the signaled rate (see Section 3.2).¶

Signaling Layer 3 rates keeps the mechanism simple. A Layer 3 rate closely corresponds to the speed perceived by users and is independent of the link-layer encapsulation, so the same value applies at the CPE, at an access node, and at the BNG, regardless of Virtual LAN (VLAN) tags or Point-to-Point Protocol over Ethernet (PPPoE) [RFC2516] headers added or removed along the path (see Section 3.1 and Section 3.3).¶

Intermediate nodes can use the same information. In many IP over Ethernet (IPoE) [TR101] architectures, the BNG operates as a DHCP relay agent. Where per-subscriber policies are not provisioned via Authentication, Authorization, and Accounting (AAA) protocols such as Remote Authentication Dial-In User Service (RADIUS) [RFC2865], the BNG can use the signaled rates to instantiate downstream shapers and upstream policers, and access nodes performing DHCP snooping can use them to configure their queues and shapers. DHCPv6 additionally allows the server to signal different rates to the client and to individual relay agents in the path (see Section 5.3).¶

1.3. Applicability

The mechanism defined in this document is primarily designed for broadband access networks using IPoE or PPPoE. In PPPoE deployments, DHCPv6 is commonly used over the Point-to-Point Protocol (PPP) [RFC1661] session for IPv6 Prefix Delegation and conveys the options in the same way (see Section 7.1). The options are defined for both DHCPv4 and DHCPv6 to support IPv4-only, IPv6-only, and dual-stack deployments.¶

DHCP is supported by virtually all CPE, including customer-owned devices and devices running open-source firmware, and is visible to intermediate nodes along the path (see Appendix A.3).¶

The options are applicable to any access link on which DHCP is used to configure the network-facing interface and on which the intended rates are determined by a subscription, contract, or policy, for example enterprise access links (see Section 7.3) and public or guest Wi-Fi networks (see Section 7.2). They convey provisioned, slowly changing values and do not describe the instantaneous capacity of links whose capacity varies over time (see Section 7.4).¶

Appendix A discusses the reasoning behind this mechanism and addresses frequently raised questions, such as why rate enforcement at the BNG alone is not sufficient, why upstream shaping is not performed at the BNG, and why only Layer 3 rates are signaled.¶

2. Conventions and Definitions

The key words "MUST", "MUST NOT", "REQUIRED", "SHALL", "SHALL NOT", "SHOULD", "SHOULD NOT", "RECOMMENDED", "NOT RECOMMENDED", "MAY", and "OPTIONAL" in this document are to be interpreted as described in BCP 14 [RFC2119] [RFC8174] when, and only when, they appear in all capitals, as shown here.¶

This document makes use of the terms defined in [RFC2131] and [RFC9915]. The following additional terms are used:¶

Binding

The DHCP configuration state of a client for a given protocol. For a client, this is the DHCPv4 lease or the Identity Associations (IAs) obtained via DHCPv6. For an intermediate node, this is the state that it maintains for a client, associating the client with its client-facing port or session and with the addresses or prefixes leased to it, for example the subscriber session of a BNG or the binding table of a snooping device.¶

Default Configuration

The configuration of the traffic management functions of a receiver for a given direction that applies when no signaled rate is in effect for that direction. It consists of any rate or traffic management policy statically configured by the administrator of the receiver for that direction or, if no such configuration exists, of no rate limitation by the receiver, i.e., traffic is forwarded up to the capacity of the physical link. The default configuration does not depend on rates previously learned from a Rate Option.¶

DHCP

The abbreviation DHCP is used throughout this document to refer to both DHCPv4 and DHCPv6 protocols.¶

DHCPv4

The Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol [RFC2131].¶

DHCPv6

The Dynamic Host Configuration Protocol for IPv6 [RFC9915].¶

Downstream

The direction of traffic from the network towards the client. For a client, this corresponds to traffic received over the link on which the Rate Option was received. For a relay agent or snooping device, this corresponds to traffic sent on the client-facing interface or port.¶

Enforcement Point

The node or link at which the provisioned rates are enforced or which constitutes the bottleneck of the path, for example a policer at the BNG or an access link.¶

Intended Rate

The maximum rate, in a given direction, at which the network intends to forward the traffic of a client. Traffic exceeding the intended rate is subject to being dropped or delayed at the enforcement point. The intended rate is not a guarantee: the achievable rate can be lower, for example due to congestion elsewhere in the network. The Rate Option signals the intended rates; this document also refers to them as the signaled rates.¶

Intermediate Node

A relay agent or snooping device on the path between a client and a server that processes the Rate Option, for example a BNG acting as a DHCP relay agent or an access node performing DHCP snooping.¶

Layer 3 Rate

A rate calculated over the outermost IP header of each packet and its payload, as transmitted on the link of the receiver (see Section 3.1).¶

Provisioned Rate

The rate configured in the network for a subscriber according to the subscribed service, typically enforced at the enforcement point (e.g., the BNG). The intended rates are typically derived from the provisioned rates.¶

Rate Option

The DHCPv4 Rate Option (OPTION_V4_RATE) or the DHCPv6 Rate Option (OPTION_V6_RATE) defined in this document. Where the distinction is not relevant, this document refers to both as the Rate Option.¶

Receiver

Any node that processes a received Rate Option, including DHCP clients, relay agents (including Lightweight DHCPv6 Relay Agents [RFC6221] and Layer 2 DHCPv4 Relay Agents [TR101]), and DHCP snooping devices.¶

Subscriber

The customer of a network access service. Within the network, a subscriber is typically represented by a subscriber session at the BNG (e.g., an IPoE or PPPoE session), to which the provisioned rates and other per-subscriber policies are applied.¶

Upstream

The direction of traffic from the client towards the network. For a client, this corresponds to traffic sent over the link on which the Rate Option was received. For a relay agent or snooping device, this corresponds to traffic received on the client-facing interface or port.¶

3. Rate Option

This document defines a Rate Option for both DHCPv4 and DHCPv6. The option code and length follow the conventions of the respective base protocol (see Section 4 and Section 5). The option value has the same fixed 16-octet format in both protocols, and its semantics, specified in this section, are identical.¶

The option value consists of two fields, in the following order:¶

Upstream Rate:

The intended Layer 3 rate for the upstream direction.¶

Downstream Rate:

The intended Layer 3 rate for the downstream direction.¶

Both fields are unsigned 64-bit integers in network byte order (big-endian), expressing the rate in bits per second (bps), not in bytes (octets) per second. Throughout this document, Mbit/s and Gbit/s denote 10^6 and 10^9 bits per second, respectively. The 64-bit format allows rates beyond the approximately 4.29 Gbit/s that can be represented by 32-bit rate attributes in bits per second, such as those defined in [RFC4679].¶

A value of 0 indicates that no rate is signaled for the respective direction. Its processing is specified in Section 3.4.¶

3.1. Layer 3 Rates

All rates signaled by the Rate Option are Layer 3 rates. A Layer 3 rate is calculated over the outermost IP header of each packet (including any IPv6 extension headers) and its payload, as transmitted or received on the link of the receiver. For a client, this is the link on which the Rate Option was received; for a relay agent or snooping device, it is the client-facing link. The Layer 3 rate excludes all headers and trailers below the outermost IP header, such as Ethernet headers, VLAN tags, PPPoE and PPP headers, and the Ethernet Frame Check Sequence (FCS), as well as all physical-layer overhead.¶

Consequently, any tunnel or translation applied by the receiver itself is part of the Layer 3 rate. For example:¶

  • For a client acting as a Dual-Stack Lite (DS-Lite) Basic Bridging BroadBand (B4) element [RFC6333] or as a Mapping of Address and Port with Encapsulation (MAP-E) Customer Edge [RFC7597], the Layer 3 rate covers the outer IPv6 header and the encapsulated IPv4 packet.¶

  • For a client using translation, such as Mapping of Address and Port using Translation (MAP-T) [RFC7599] or the customer-side translator of 464XLAT [RFC6877], the Layer 3 rate covers the translated packet as transmitted on the link.¶

  • For a client that encapsulates traffic in an IP-based tunnel on the link of the receiver, such as an IPsec, Generic Routing Encapsulation (GRE), or Layer 2 Tunneling Protocol (L2TP) tunnel, the Layer 3 rate covers the outer IP header and all headers and payload carried within it.¶

A receiver that applies its traffic management functions to the inner traffic of such a tunnel or translation, rather than to the packets on the link, accounts for the resulting difference locally, as the encapsulation or translation is performed by the receiver itself. Encapsulations added or removed by other nodes between the receiver and the enforcement point, such as an L2TP tunnel between an L2TP Access Concentrator (LAC) and an L2TP Network Server (LNS) [RFC2661], are not part of the Layer 3 rate of the receiver; they are treated like any other difference in accounting at the enforcement point (see Section 8).¶

Layer 3 rates have the following properties:¶

  • They closely correspond to the speed perceived by users and to the rate in which service tiers are typically described and marketed. Speed test applications typically measure the throughput of the transport layer, which is lower than the Layer 3 rate by the share of the IP and transport headers, for example by approximately 3 to 5% for full-size packets.¶

  • They are independent of the packet size. A Layer 3 rate does not include any per-packet overhead below the IP header, so a shaper that accounts the IP packet size enforces the same rate for small and large packets.¶

  • They are independent of the link-layer encapsulation used on the link of the receiver and on any other segment of the path. Unless an IP-based tunnel is added or removed between the receiver and the enforcement point, a single value applies at every node, regardless of VLAN tags or PPPoE headers that are added or removed along the path.¶

  • They can be applied directly by traffic management functions that account traffic at the IP layer, such as shapers attached to a PPP or IP interface, and by traffic management functions that compensate for the link-layer headers of the local interface (see Section 3.3).¶

The Rate Option does not describe the per-packet accounting of the enforcement point. Operators account for differences between the Layer 3 rate and the accounting of the enforcement point when configuring the signaled rates or the enforcement point (see Section 8).¶

3.2. Traffic Management Using Signaled Rates

The Rate Option conveys rates that are intended to be applied to local traffic management functions, such as traffic shapers, policers, or AQM parameters. A client that supports such traffic management functions SHOULD apply the upstream rate, unless it treats the signaled rates as information only for one of the reasons listed below, and MAY apply the downstream rate. Relay agents and snooping devices MAY apply the rates in either direction (see Section 4, Section 5, and Section 6).¶

From the perspective of a client, the upstream rate is the relevant one: downstream traffic is typically already shaped by the network before it reaches the client, whereas upstream traffic is enforced by the network only after it has left the client. Where the downstream shaper at the BNG does not apply AQM, a client can additionally shape received traffic slightly below the downstream rate to control queuing delay. Intermediate nodes typically apply the rates in both directions.¶

A receiver whose traffic management functions account traffic at the IP layer applies the signaled rate directly. A receiver whose traffic management functions account link-layer frames requires header compensation to apply a Layer 3 rate precisely, as specified in Section 3.3.¶

A client can receive a rate that exceeds the capacity of the physical link of the respective interface (e.g., 2 Gbit/s signaled to a CPE with a 1 Gbit/s Wide Area Network (WAN) port). A client SHOULD cap the applied traffic shaping, policing, or AQM parameters to the capacity of the physical link. On links with variable capacity, the effective rate is the lower of the signaled rate and the current link capacity (see Section 7.4).¶

Relay agents and snooping devices apply signaled rates to the traffic of an individual client. This document assumes that each such client is associated with a dedicated physical or logical client-facing port, such as an access line or a per-client VLAN (e.g., the 1:1 VLAN model described in [TR101]). If multiple clients share the same client-facing port, and the device is not able to apply rates to the traffic of individual clients, the device SHOULD NOT apply the signaled rates to that port.¶

If both the client and an intermediate node that enforces the signaled rates account traffic at Layer 3, a client that shapes its traffic to the signaled rate does not trigger the enforcement at the intermediate node, and no correction factor is required. Intermediate nodes MAY nevertheless apply an implementation-specific correction factor, for example by enforcing a rate slightly above the signaled rate (e.g., by 1%), to ensure that they do not drop packets of a compliant client, for example due to minor burstiness or measurement differences, while they still enforce the provisioned rate for clients that do not support or ignore this option. An intermediate node that accounts link-layer overhead requires a correction factor that additionally covers that overhead (see Section 8). Alternatively, in DHCPv6, the server can signal node-specific rates (see Section 5.3).¶

A receiver that does not apply a signaled rate treats it as information only, for example because it does not support the required traffic management functions, because its processing capacity is not sufficient to apply traffic management at the signaled rate, because the signaled rates do not apply to most of its traffic, such as in networks with substantial local traffic (see Section 7.2), or because applying the downstream rate is optional for clients. Such a receiver can expose the rate in its management interfaces (see Section 8).¶

The signaled rates do not account for other bottlenecks along the path to a remote peer. Receivers MUST NOT use the signaled rates as a replacement for congestion control or other adaptive rate mechanisms, and MUST NOT use them to increase the sending rate of a congestion control algorithm, such as its initial sending rate, beyond the rate that the algorithm would otherwise use. Where rate limits need to be enforced, enforcement remains the responsibility of the network; the Rate Option only informs receivers about such limits.¶

Explicit configuration by the administrator of the receiver, such as disabling the application of signaled rates or configuring fixed rates or per-packet overhead values, takes precedence over the signaled rates. In the absence of such configuration, the signaled rates take precedence over the default configuration of the receiver.¶

3.3. Link-Layer Header Compensation

Traffic management functions frequently account the size of each packet as it is seen on the interface to which they are attached. A shaper attached to a PPP or IP interface accounts the IP packet and therefore enforces a Layer 3 rate without further action. A shaper attached to an Ethernet interface, however, typically accounts the Ethernet frame, including the link-layer headers added by the receiver itself, such as the Ethernet header (14 octets), any VLAN tags (4 octets each), and the PPPoE (6 octets) and PPP (2 octets) headers. Applying a Layer 3 rate to such a shaper requires header compensation.¶

Header compensation means that the traffic management function accounts each packet with the size of its IP packet rather than the size of the link-layer frame, i.e., it subtracts the size of the link-layer headers from the accounted size of each packet. As these headers are added by the receiver itself, their size is known locally and does not need to be signaled. The headers to subtract are those that the traffic management function actually accounts, which can differ from the headers transmitted on the link, for example if VLAN tags are inserted by hardware after the traffic management function or if the Ethernet Frame Check Sequence (FCS) is not accounted.¶

For example, a CPE using PPPoE over a VLAN-tagged Ethernet interface whose shaper accounts the Ethernet frame without FCS compensates 26 octets per packet (14 octets Ethernet header, 4 octets VLAN tag, 6 octets PPPoE header, and 2 octets PPP header). With this compensation, the shaper enforces exactly the signaled Layer 3 rate for any packet size. Common shapers support such compensation, for example through a configurable, possibly negative, per-packet overhead, or, in the case of the Common Applications Kept Enhanced (CAKE) queue discipline, by accounting the network-layer packet size when an overhead is configured (see Section 9).¶

A receiver whose traffic management functions account link-layer frames SHOULD perform header compensation. A receiver that does not support header compensation MAY apply the signaled rate to its link-layer accounting directly. As each accounted frame is larger than the IP packet it carries, the resulting effective Layer 3 rate is lower than the signaled rate, which does not trigger the enforcement at the enforcement point. The reduction, however, depends on the packet size: with 26 octets of link-layer headers, the effective Layer 3 rate is approximately 98% of the signaled rate for 1500-octet packets, 79% for 100-octet packets, and 61% for 40-octet packets, such as TCP acknowledgments. Header compensation avoids this packet-size-dependent loss of throughput.¶

3.4. Rate Processing

Rates are maintained per link and per direction. As DHCPv4 and DHCPv6 can both convey the Rate Option on the same link, a receiver maintains two independent rate sets per link: the rates learned via DHCPv4 and the rates learned via DHCPv6. Each rate set consists of an upstream and a downstream rate and follows the binding of the respective protocol. Both rate sets apply to the same link, even if DHCPv4 and DHCPv6 are processed on different logical interfaces of the receiver.¶

A rate set is created, replaced, and cleared as follows:¶

  • Each valid Rate Option received in a DHCPv4 DHCPACK message replaces the DHCPv4 rate set. Each valid Rate Option received in a DHCPv6 Reply message (or, for DHCPv6 relay agents, in the corresponding Relay-reply message) replaces the DHCPv6 rate set.¶

  • A valid Rate Option in which both values are 0 clears the rate set of the respective protocol. This is the only way for a server to remove previously signaled rates while the binding persists.¶

  • If a DHCPACK or Reply message does not contain a valid Rate Option, the receiver MUST retain the rate set of the respective protocol unchanged. This includes DHCPv6 Reply messages sent in response to Confirm, Release, or Decline messages, which do not carry configuration options.¶

  • The receiver MUST clear the rate set of a protocol when the corresponding binding ends, i.e., for DHCPv4, when the lease expires or is released, and, for DHCPv6, when all IAs obtained in the exchange that carried the Rate Option have expired, have been released, or have been declined.¶

  • A client using stateless DHCPv6 [RFC9915] has no DHCPv6 binding. Its DHCPv6 rate set remains in effect until it is replaced or cleared by a Rate Option in a subsequent Information-request exchange.¶

  • The receiver MUST clear both rate sets when the underlying link or session (e.g., the PPPoE session) is terminated.¶

The rates in effect are derived from the two rate sets whenever one of them changes:¶

  • If only one rate set exists, the receiver uses it.¶

  • If both rate sets exist, the receiver SHOULD use the DHCPv6 rate set. As operators configure identical rates for both protocols (see below), the two rate sets differ only transiently, and receivers MAY apply a different local policy. The selected rate set is used for both directions; rates of different rate sets are not combined.¶

  • If no rate set exists, the receiver reverts to its default configuration for both directions (see Section 2).¶

For each direction of the selected rate set, if the value is non-zero, the receiver applies the signaled rate as specified in Section 3.2, subject to the sanity check below. If the value is 0, the receiver MUST remove any rate previously applied for that direction and revert to its default configuration for that direction.¶

A client SHOULD treat a non-zero rate that is too low to ensure basic connectivity as if it were 0, i.e., revert to its default configuration for that direction. The threshold depends on the deployment and the use case of the device and is therefore left to the implementation, which can allow the administrator or operator to configure it. For example, a broadband CPE might ignore rates below 1 Mbit/s. Rates above the capacity of the physical link are capped as specified in Section 3.2.¶

In some deployments, DHCP renewals occur frequently, and most of them carry unchanged rates. If a rate in effect is identical to the rate currently applied, the receiver SHOULD NOT reconfigure its traffic management functions for that direction. Comparing the newly derived rates with the stored rates before any reconfiguration is sufficient to achieve this.¶

The state required by these rules is small: two rate sets per link, each cleared together with the binding of its protocol. It can be maintained by simple event-driven scripts on the client (see Section 9).¶

Intermediate nodes apply the same rules per client, using the rates they learn for that client via each protocol. They SHOULD clear the rate set of a protocol when the corresponding binding of the client is removed. An intermediate node that does not maintain bindings removes the rates applied for a client when the client-facing port or session is terminated.¶

In dual-stack deployments, DHCPv4 and DHCPv6 are frequently served by independent servers, for example a centralized DHCPv4 server and a DHCPv6 server co-located with the BNG, which cannot inspect each other's state. Network operators MUST ensure that the DHCPv4 and DHCPv6 services for the same client are configured with identical intended rates, or that only one of the two protocols, which is expected to be negotiated by every client, conveys the Rate Option. In the latter case, the rate set of the other protocol never exists, and the rates in effect are determined by the protocol that conveys the Rate Option alone.¶

Even with identical configuration, the two protocols can transiently convey different rates, for example while a rate change is being provisioned on both servers, or because the renewal times of DHCPv4 and DHCPv6 are not aligned. As receivers prefer the DHCPv6 rate set, the rates in effect do not alternate in this case: a change conveyed via DHCPv6 takes effect immediately, and a change conveyed via DHCPv4 takes effect once the DHCPv6 rate set reflects it or no longer exists. Operators can shorten such transitions by updating the rates on both servers together and, where supported, by triggering an immediate update via DHCPFORCERENEW or Reconfigure (see Section 4.3 and Section 5.3).¶

3.5. Error Handling

A Rate Option whose value is not 16 octets long is malformed. A receiver MUST discard a malformed Rate Option, which is then treated as if it were not present, i.e., the rate set of the respective protocol remains unchanged (see Section 3.4).¶

A sender MUST NOT include more than one instance of the Rate Option in a DHCPv4 message, or at the same encapsulation level of a DHCPv6 message (i.e., within the message exchanged with the client or within a given Relay-reply message).¶

A DHCPv4 receiver that receives multiple instances of the Rate Option concatenates them as specified in [RFC3396] and validates the length of the concatenated value. Multiple complete instances therefore result in a malformed value. A DHCPv6 receiver that receives more than one instance at the same encapsulation level MUST discard all instances at that level.¶

A Rate Option contained in a message other than those listed in Section 4 and Section 5 MUST be ignored.¶

4. DHCPv4

4.1. DHCPv4 Rate Option

The DHCPv4 Rate Option (OPTION_V4_RATE) has the following format:¶

Code:

TBD1.¶

Length:

16.¶

Upstream Rate, Downstream Rate:

Unsigned 64-bit integers in network byte order (big-endian), in bits per second (bps), as specified in Section 3.¶

The DHCPv4 Rate Option can appear in the following messages:¶

Table 1: Appearance of the DHCPv4 Rate Option
Message Use
DHCPOFFER Server selection (see Section 4.2)
DHCPACK Rate information (see Section 3.4)

4.2. DHCPv4 Client Behavior

DHCPv4 clients that support the Rate Option SHOULD include its code in the Parameter Request List (PRL) of every DHCPDISCOVER, DHCPREQUEST, and DHCPINFORM message. This signals client support, enabling the DHCPv4 server to determine whether to include the Rate Option in its response, so that updated rates reach the client with every renewal. Furthermore, it provides network operators with visibility into client capabilities, which aids in troubleshooting and resolving customer service quality complaints.¶

A client MUST NOT apply rates received in any DHCPv4 message other than DHCPACK. A client MAY use the rates contained in a DHCPOFFER message as a criterion for selecting among multiple offers. Rates received in a DHCPACK message are processed as specified in Section 3.4.¶

4.3. DHCPv4 Server Behavior

When a DHCPv4 server that supports the Rate Option receives a client message whose PRL includes the code of the Rate Option, and a rate is configured for the client for at least one direction, the server MUST include the Rate Option in the resulting DHCPOFFER and DHCPACK messages, with a value of 0 for a direction for which no rate is configured. As an absent Rate Option leaves the rates in effect unchanged, a server removes previously signaled rates by including the Rate Option with a value of 0 for the respective directions (see Section 3.4).¶

The server MAY derive the rates from local configuration profiles, centralized AAA systems such as RADIUS, or external policy servers.¶

A server MAY include the Rate Option in its responses even if the client did not request it via the PRL, provided the operator has explicitly configured the server to do so, for example to provision Layer 2 DHCPv4 Relay Agents or snooping devices.¶

To update the rates of a client before its next scheduled renewal, a server MAY send a DHCPFORCERENEW message [RFC3203], causing the client to initiate a renewal transaction. DHCPFORCERENEW messages are subject to the authentication requirements of [RFC3203], which can be satisfied using the mechanism specified in [RFC6704]. For clients that do not support DHCPFORCERENEW, updated rates take effect with the next renewal, the timing of which is controlled by the renewal (T1) time value.¶

4.4. DHCPv4 Relay Agent Behavior

DHCPv4 relay agents, including Layer 2 DHCPv4 Relay Agents [TR101], MAY extract the Rate Option from DHCPACK messages traversing the network and use them to instantiate shapers and policers on their client-facing interfaces. Layer 2 DHCPv4 Relay Agents MUST only process a Rate Option contained in DHCPACK messages received on trusted, network-facing ports.¶

Apart from removing the Relay Agent Information option [RFC3046], a DHCPv4 relay agent does not change the options of a server message destined for the client [RFC2131]. Consequently, a relay agent does not insert, replace, modify, or remove a Rate Option in that message. A device that provides the client with rates derived from subscriber attributes (e.g., retrieved from an AAA server such as RADIUS), for example a BNG, acts as the DHCPv4 server for the client and includes these rates when constructing its responses, as specified in Section 4.3. Devices that rewrite the message destined for the client while acting as a relay agent are outside the scope of this document.¶

5.1. DHCPv6 Rate Option

The DHCPv6 Rate Option (OPTION_V6_RATE) has the following format:¶

option-code:

TBD2.¶

option-len:

16.¶

Upstream Rate, Downstream Rate:

Unsigned 64-bit integers in network byte order (big-endian), in bits per second (bps), as specified in Section 3.¶

The DHCPv6 Rate Option can appear in the following messages:¶

Table 2: Appearance of the DHCPv6 Rate Option
Message Use
Advertise Server selection (see Section 5.2)
Reply Rate information for the client (see Section 3.4)
Relay-reply Rate information for the relay agent (see Section 5.4)

The Rate Option is included in Reply messages sent in response to Solicit, Request, Renew, Rebind, and Information-request messages. It is not included in Reconfigure messages, as specified in Section 18.3.11 of [RFC9915].¶

5.2. DHCPv6 Client Behavior

DHCPv6 clients that support the Rate Option SHOULD include its code in the Option Request option (ORO) [RFC9915] of every Solicit, Request, Renew, Rebind, and Information-request message, for the same reasons as specified for DHCPv4 in Section 4.2.¶

A client MUST NOT apply rates received in any DHCPv6 message other than Reply. A client MAY use the rates contained in an Advertise message as a criterion for selecting among multiple Advertise messages. Rates received in a Reply message are processed as specified in Section 3.4.¶

5.3. DHCPv6 Server Behavior

When a DHCPv6 server that supports the Rate Option receives a Solicit, Request, Renew, Rebind, or Information-request message whose ORO includes the code of the Rate Option, and a rate is configured for the client for at least one direction, the server MUST include the Rate Option in the resulting Advertise and Reply messages, with a value of 0 for a direction for which no rate is configured. As an absent Rate Option leaves the rates in effect unchanged, a server removes previously signaled rates by including the Rate Option with a value of 0 for the respective directions (see Section 3.4).¶

A server MAY include the Rate Option in its responses even if the client did not request it via the ORO, provided the operator has explicitly configured the server to do so.¶

In relayed exchanges, the Rate Option for the client is included in the Reply message, which is encapsulated in one or more Relay-reply messages. The server MAY additionally include the Rate Option in a Relay-reply message to provision the corresponding relay agent. The nested Relay-reply structure of DHCPv6 allows the server to address each relay agent in the path individually, in addition to the client. As DHCPv6 does not define a mechanism for relay agents to request options from the server, the server includes the Rate Option in a Relay-reply message based on local configuration, for example by identifying the relay agent by the link-address or peer-address field of the Relay-forward message, or by relay agent options such as Interface-Id [RFC9915] or Remote-Id [RFC4649].¶

Including the Rate Option in a Relay-reply message is OPTIONAL for servers, and processing it is OPTIONAL for relay agents. Relay agents that do not support this document are expected to ignore the option, as they do not recognize it. Relay agents that do not receive rates in this way can instead obtain them by inspecting the Rate Option destined for the client, as specified for snooping devices in Section 6, or through other means, such as AAA.¶

When provisioning multiple encapsulation levels, the server MAY supply different rates to each node. For example, an operator might configure the upstream policer of a relay agent with a slightly higher rate than the upstream shaper of the CPE, to accommodate minor burstiness of the CPE traffic. DHCPv4 does not provide a comparable mechanism for addressing multiple relay agents individually.¶

To update the rates of a client before its next scheduled renewal, a server MAY send a Reconfigure message as specified in Section 18.3.11 of [RFC9915], causing the client to initiate a Renew, Rebind, or Information-request transaction. As specified in [RFC9915], a server can only send Reconfigure messages to clients that have included the Reconfigure Accept option in their messages, and Reconfigure messages are authenticated using the Reconfiguration Key Authentication Protocol (RKAP; see Section 20.4 of [RFC9915]). The server MAY include the code of the Rate Option in the Option Request option of the Reconfigure message to indicate that the rates have changed.¶

For clients that do not support Reconfigure, updated rates take effect with the next Renew or Rebind transaction or, for stateless clients, with the next Information-request transaction. Operators can control the timing of such updates through the T1 value and the Information Refresh Time option (Section 21.23 of [RFC9915]).¶

5.4. DHCPv6 Relay Agent Behavior

DHCPv6 relay agents that support the Rate Option, including Lightweight DHCPv6 Relay Agents (LDRA) [RFC6221], process a Rate Option contained in a Relay-reply message addressed to them as specified in Section 3.4. Such relay agents MAY use the signaled rates to instantiate shapers, policers, or AQM disciplines on their client-facing interfaces. Relay agents MUST only process Relay-reply messages received from the network side; in particular, an LDRA discards Relay-reply messages received on client-facing interfaces as specified in Section 6.1.1 of [RFC6221].¶

A Rate Option contained in the encapsulated message destined for the client targets the client only. Relay agents MUST NOT use such an option to configure their own traffic management, since the DHCPv6 relay architecture provides the Relay-reply message as a dedicated channel for signaling rates to relay agents.¶

A DHCPv6 relay agent does not change the message encapsulated in the Relay Message option (Section 19.2 of [RFC9915] and Section 19.4 of [RFC9915]). Consequently, a relay agent does not insert, replace, modify, or remove a Rate Option in the message destined for the client. A device that provides the client with rates derived from subscriber attributes, for example a BNG, acts as the DHCPv6 server for the client and includes these rates when constructing its responses, as specified in Section 5.3. Devices that rewrite the encapsulated message while acting as a relay agent are outside the scope of this document.¶

6. DHCP Snooping

DHCP snooping devices are typically deployed as intermediate Layer 2 devices that passively monitor DHCP message exchanges to enforce security policies and build binding databases, without modifying the DHCP payloads. These devices MAY inspect the Rate Option in messages destined for clients and use them to provision traffic shapers, policers, or hardware queues on the corresponding client-facing ports.¶

Snooping devices MUST only process a Rate Option contained in DHCPACK (DHCPv4) and Reply (DHCPv6) messages received on trusted, network-facing ports. A Rate Option contained in a message received on a client-facing port MUST NOT be used to configure traffic management.¶

7. Deployment Scenarios

7.1. PPPoE

In PPPoE [RFC2516] architectures, the CPE typically employs DHCPv6 over the PPP link to obtain an IPv6 prefix via Prefix Delegation (IA_PD) [RFC9915]. This DHCPv6 exchange conveys the Rate Option in the same way as in IPoE deployments. DHCPv4 transactions within a PPPoE session can similarly convey the DHCPv4 Rate Option.¶

The processing rules for a Rate Option received over PPPoE are identical to those defined for IPoE, as specified in Section 3.4. In particular, the rates in effect revert to the default configuration of the device when the PPPoE session is terminated.¶

As the signaled rates are Layer 3 rates, a CPE that attaches its shaper to the PPP interface applies them directly, without accounting for the PPPoE, PPP, Ethernet, or VLAN headers.¶

If a client receives rate information from other sources, for example within PPP authentication messages, and also receives a Rate Option, the client SHOULD give precedence to the Rate Option. This ensures that rate information that is refreshed with every DHCP renewal supersedes rate information that is conveyed only once per session.¶

7.2. Local Area Networks

The Rate Option can also be used by DHCP servers in local area networks, for example a DHCP server co-located with a CPE or another gateway, to inform clients about the rates available to them towards the Internet or another upstream network. In this case, the signaled rates indicate the maximum rate a client can expect through the upstream connection of the network it is attached to.¶

This is primarily useful in networks that are used mainly for Internet access, such as public Wi-Fi hotspots and guest networks, where the upstream connection is the relevant bottleneck for most of the traffic of a client. In networks that are also used for substantial local traffic, such as home or corporate networks with local servers, printers, or storage, the signaled rates do not apply to the local traffic. Clients in such networks SHOULD NOT apply the signaled rates to traffic towards on-link destinations, and typically use the signaled rates as information only (see Section 3.2).¶

Examples include:¶

  • Virtual Private Network (VPN) gateways or tunnel endpoints that shape traffic towards their peers and whose processing capacity exceeds the capacity of the upstream connection.¶

  • Public Wi-Fi hotspots that enforce per-user rate limits, where clients can align their sending rate with the enforced limit to avoid policer drops.¶

  • End devices, such as smartphones, laptops, or other Wi-Fi clients, that display the offered upstream connection rates to the user, for example in the details of the connected network, or that use them as an initial estimate, e.g., to estimate transfer times, to select an initial video quality, or to decide whether to keep using the network or to prefer another available interface, such as a cellular connection.¶

End devices decide whether they also apply the signaled rates to their traffic management functions or use them as information only, as specified in Section 3.2.¶

As DHCP is performed after association with a wireless network, the signaled rates are only available once the device has connected. A device can retain the rates learned for a network and take them into account when selecting among known networks later. Providing information about the capacity of a wireless network prior to association is outside the scope of this document.¶

A device that acts as a DHCP client on its network-facing interface and as a DHCP server on its LAN interfaces MAY derive the rates it signals to LAN clients from the rates learned on its network-facing interface, from local configuration, or both. In this case, it SHOULD signal the lower of the learned rate and any locally configured limit for the client. When the learned rates change, the device SHOULD signal the updated rates to its LAN clients with their next renewal and MAY trigger an earlier update as specified in Section 4.3 and Section 5.3. As Layer 3 rates are independent of the link-layer encapsulation, the learned rates can typically be passed on unchanged.¶

Unless the signaled rates are enforced for each client individually, they describe a capacity shared by all clients of the LAN. A client that shapes its traffic to such a rate does not exceed the shared capacity on its own, but it does not obtain a guaranteed share of it either.¶

Clients cap the applied rates to the capacity of their physical link, and the signaled rates do not replace congestion control (see Section 3.2).¶

7.3. Enterprise Access Links

Enterprise branch offices are often connected via access links whose contracted rate is lower than the speed of the physical interface, for example a 100 Mbit/s service delivered over a 1 Gbit/s Ethernet interface, with the contracted rate enforced by a policer in the provider network. Where the provider uses DHCP to configure the customer edge router, the Rate Option allows the router, including Software-Defined Wide Area Network (SD-WAN) edge devices, to configure its upstream shaper without manual provisioning.¶

Where the service defines both a committed and an excess or peak rate, the intended rate is the maximum rate at which the provider intends to forward traffic (see Section 2). Bandwidth profiles of such services commonly account Ethernet frames; the considerations for enforcement points that account traffic at Layer 2 apply (see Section 8).¶

7.4. Links with Variable Capacity

On wireless, cellular, and satellite access links, the available capacity varies with radio conditions, scheduling, and load on time scales far shorter than DHCP lease and renewal intervals. The Rate Option is not suitable for conveying such variations. It can, however, convey a provisioned upper bound, for example a rate limit defined by the subscription. Receivers on such links use the lower of the signaled rate and the current link capacity (see Section 3.2). Where the bottleneck is the variable-capacity link itself, a shaper configured with the signaled rate does not prevent queuing at that link; receivers can then use the signaled rate as an upper bound for mechanisms that adapt to the current capacity, such as shapers that adjust their rate based on measured delay or throughput.¶

8. Operational Considerations

Deploying explicit rate signaling via DHCP concentrates upstream congestion at the CPE, where it can be managed by AQM and, where deployed, L4S-compatible queue management, and thereby minimizes queuing delay in the access segment.¶

Operators typically signal the IP-layer rate the subscriber is intended to achieve, which is commonly the rate of the subscribed service as described to the subscriber. As Layer 3 rates are independent of the encapsulation, the same value can be signaled to the CPE and to intermediate nodes (see Section 3.1).¶

Where supported, enforcement points that apply the signaled rates, such as a policer at the BNG, are configured to account traffic at Layer 3, i.e., excluding link-layer headers. In combination with receivers that perform header compensation (see Section 3.3), the signaled rate is then enforced consistently for all packet sizes, and only the optional correction factor described in Section 3.2 applies.¶

Where the enforcement point accounts link-layer overhead, or where the bottleneck is a physical link that consumes per-packet overhead, such as encapsulation headers, the Ethernet FCS, preamble, and inter-packet gap, the difference to the Layer 3 rate depends on the packet size. For example, the Ethernet, VLAN, PPPoE, and PPP headers add 26 octets to each IP packet, which corresponds to approximately 1.7% for 1500-octet packets but to a considerably larger share for small packets. Operators account for this difference when configuring the enforcement point or the signaled rate, for example by configuring the enforcement point with a margin above the signaled rate that covers this overhead for typical packet sizes, or by signaling a correspondingly lower Layer 3 rate. As the overhead is per packet, such a margin or reduction is exact only for a given packet size. Traffic consisting predominantly of small packets can still exceed such an enforcement point; this residual inaccuracy is accepted in exchange for the simplicity of a single, encapsulation-independent parameter (see Appendix A).¶

Where the actual rate of the access line is known and lower than the provisioned rate, for example due to the synchronization rate of a DSL line, operators are advised to signal the lower of the two rates, so that the client shapes its traffic to the actual bottleneck. The actual rate is typically known at the BNG, for example from the Actual-Data-Rate-Upstream and Actual-Data-Rate-Downstream attributes [RFC4679] or the corresponding Access Node Control Protocol (ANCP) Type-Length-Value (TLV) elements [RFC6320]. As these attributes describe the net data rate of the access line, the server converts them to a Layer 3 rate, for example by deducting the encapsulation overhead for typical packet sizes and, for lines using Packet Transfer Mode (PTM) with 64/65-octet encapsulation, the corresponding 1/65 of the rate. Access lines with cell-based link layers, such as Asynchronous Transfer Mode (ATM) on Asymmetric Digital Subscriber Line (ADSL), have a packet-size-dependent overhead that a single rate cannot describe precisely; operators of such lines can signal a conservatively reduced rate, and precise modeling remains a matter of local configuration of the CPE.¶

Without the use of DHCPFORCERENEW or Reconfigure, changes of the provisioned rates take effect with the next renewal of the client. Operators are advised to consider this delay when choosing lease and renewal times, for example when rates are changed due to service upgrades or fair-use policies.¶

The inclusion of the code of the Rate Option in the Parameter Request List or Option Request option allows operators to determine the share of clients supporting this option, for example to plan the introduction of correction factors or to identify clients that require enforcement at the network.¶

Implementations are encouraged to expose the signaled rates within CPE management interfaces, such as the local Web User Interface (UI) or remote management protocols. As Layer 3 rates closely match the results of speed tests (see Section 3.1), this can reduce support inquiries related to perceived bandwidth issues.¶

As noted in Section 3.2, a client can receive a rate that exceeds the capacity of the physical link of its interfaces. In such scenarios, management interfaces can expose both the signaled rate and the effective, capped rate applied by the device, and implementations can log this discrepancy if logging facilities are enabled. Such events can indicate a mismatch between the subscriber's provisioned service tier and the installed equipment.¶

9.1. Implementation Feasibility on Open-Source CPE

The authors analyzed the feasibility of implementing the client behavior on OpenWrt without modifying the DHCP client software. The DHCPv4 client (BusyBox udhcpc) and DHCPv6 client (odhcp6c) used by OpenWrt can request the Rate Option and expose received options of unknown type as hexadecimal environment variables to user-defined hook scripts. Both clients also invoke these scripts when the binding ends: udhcpc with the "deconfig" event, for example when the lease expires or is released, and odhcp6c with the "unbound" or "stopped" event. All client behavior specified in this document can therefore be implemented in such scripts. On each event, a script splits the 16-octet value into two integers, stores the rate set of its protocol in a small file, or deletes that file if both values are 0 or the binding has ended, leaving the file unchanged if the Rate Option is absent, derives the rates in effect from the stored rate sets as specified in Section 3.4, and reconfigures local traffic management only if the result differs from the rates currently applied, for example via Smart Queue Management (SQM) or the CAKE queue discipline. As the signaled rates are Layer 3 rates, they can be passed to CAKE directly as its bandwidth parameter, together with an overhead of 0, with which CAKE accounts the network-layer packet size and thereby performs the header compensation described in Section 3.3. The rates learned on the WAN interface can be propagated to LAN clients using the existing custom-option support of the OpenWrt DHCPv4 server (dnsmasq) and DHCPv6 server (odhcpd).¶

10. Security Considerations

DHCP messages are typically transmitted as plaintext and are unauthenticated. Consequently, the Rate Option defined in this document is vulnerable to interception, modification, or spoofing by on-path attackers. This applies to any path the options traverse, including the path between servers and clients as well as the path between servers and relay agents, where a Rate Option carried in a Relay-reply message configures the relay agent itself (see Section 5.4). A malicious actor or a rogue DHCP server could inject artificially low rates to throttle a client's connection, resulting in a localized Denial of Service (DoS).¶

The severity of this risk is generally no greater than that of standard DHCP threat vectors, such as rogue default gateway assignment, DNS hijacking, or IP pool exhaustion, which typically yield a much more critical and immediate loss of service.¶

Operators can mitigate rogue DHCP servers by deploying DHCP snooping for DHCPv4 and DHCPv6-Shield [RFC7610] for DHCPv6 on access nodes, which discard server messages received on client-facing ports. These mechanisms protect both the clients and the intermediate nodes that apply signaled rates.¶

To mitigate the impact of malicious, spoofed, or malformed options, clients ignore implausibly low rates (see Section 3.4) and cap rates above the capacity of the physical link (see Section 3.2).¶

Conversely, an attacker could inject an excessively high rate or a rate of 0, thereby removing any effective rate limit. Because clients cap the effective rate to the capacity of the physical link (see Section 3.2), the impact is limited to the loss of the benefits of rate signaling, such as increased queuing delay at the bottleneck, which corresponds to the behavior of a device without support for this option. As receivers prefer the DHCPv6 rate set (see Section 3.4), a spoofed DHCPv6 message overrides rates learned via DHCPv4; this does not introduce a new attack, as an attacker able to spoof DHCPv6 messages can equally spoof DHCPv4 messages. Where an intermediate node derives the enforcement of the provisioned rate from the Rate Option (see Section 1.2), however, an attacker on the path between the server and that node that raises or removes the signaled rates also raises or removes the enforcement, allowing a client to exceed its provisioned rate. Intermediate nodes that derive the enforcement of the provisioned rate from the Rate Option SHOULD do so only if the messages exchanged between the server and the intermediate node are protected against modification, for example using IPsec as specified in [RFC8213] for DHCPv4 and DHCPv6, or are exchanged within a trusted administrative domain whose paths are not accessible to subscribers. Where neither applies, operators provision the enforcement of the provisioned rate by other means, such as AAA, and use the Rate Option only to inform the client.¶

In local area networks, DHCP snooping is often not deployed, making rogue DHCP servers more likely. A rogue server signaling artificially low rates could cause applications on end devices to reduce their quality or to prefer another network interface, which can incur additional cost (e.g., cellular data). Conversely, a rogue server advertising excessively high rates in DHCPOFFER or Advertise messages could be preferred by clients that use rates to select among servers (see Section 4.2 and Section 5.2). Clients SHOULD NOT use the signaled rates as the sole criterion for selecting a network interface or a server, and the mitigations described above apply.¶

Relay agents and snooping devices that instantiate traffic management functions based on the Rate Option consume finite resources, such as hardware queues and shapers. Because only server messages received on trusted ports are processed and rates are applied per client-facing port (see Section 3.2), the amount of such resources is bounded by the number of client-facing ports. However, a client can trigger frequent DHCP exchanges, potentially causing repeated reconfiguration of these functions. In addition to not reconfiguring unchanged rates (see Section 3.4), implementations MAY rate-limit DHCP messages received on client-facing ports or the reconfiguration of traffic management functions.¶

11. Privacy Considerations

The general privacy considerations for DHCP specified in [RFC7844] apply to this document.¶

The Rate Option conveys the rates provisioned for a client, which typically reflect the service tier of a subscriber. Because DHCP messages are typically transmitted as plaintext, this information is exposed to on-path observers, as well as to relay agents and snooping devices that process the options. The rate information itself does not identify a subscriber, but in combination with other information it can reveal commercial details of the service contract.¶

Clients that include the code of the Rate Option in the Parameter Request List or Option Request option disclose additional information that can contribute to fingerprinting the device. Clients that implement the anonymity profiles specified in [RFC7844] MAY omit the code of the Rate Option from the Parameter Request List or Option Request option.¶

The operational practice of exposing and logging signaled and effective rates (see Section 8) can provide operators with information about the equipment installed by a subscriber. Such data is subject to the operator's applicable privacy policies.¶

Appendix A. Design Rationale

This appendix summarizes the reasoning behind the mechanism defined in this document and addresses questions that are frequently raised.¶

A.1. Why Is Rate Enforcement at the BNG Not Sufficient?

Rate enforcement at the BNG ensures that a subscriber does not exceed the provisioned rate, but not that the subscriber can use this rate efficiently. Upstream policers cause bursty losses and unstable throughput, the upstream bottleneck can be located at an access device whose queue is not managed, and prioritization at the CPE remains ineffective as long as the CPE is not the bottleneck (see Section 1.1). Shaping at the CPE is the only practical means to address all of these.¶

Shaping at the CPE complements the enforcement at the BNG: the BNG continues to enforce the provisioned rate, optionally with a small correction factor (see Section 3.2), while the CPE ensures that this enforcement is not triggered under normal operation. CPE that do not support this option continue to be subject to the enforcement at the BNG, so that the provisioned service tier is maintained in all cases.¶

A.2. Why Not Shape Upstream Traffic at the BNG?

Shaping requires queuing. Some forwarding hardware used for BNGs does not support per-subscriber queuing in the upstream direction at all. On forwarding hardware based on virtual output queues, for example, queues are associated with a destination port and traffic class rather than with the subscriber from which the traffic is received. Where per-subscriber upstream queuing is supported, it is commonly restricted: depending on the platform, it is available only on specific line cards or ports, requires the forwarding hardware to operate in a dedicated mode, or consumes the same queuing, buffer, and scheduling resources that are required for downstream hierarchical QoS, thereby reducing the overall subscriber scale. Alternatives such as the recirculation of upstream traffic through the egress pipeline reduce the forwarding capacity. For these reasons, upstream enforcement is commonly implemented using policers, even on platforms that support ingress queuing.¶

Furthermore, even where upstream shaping at the BNG is available, the BNG has limited knowledge of the traffic it shapes. Traffic from multiple devices behind the CPE typically shares a single address due to Network Address Translation (NAT), so that prioritization of individual clients or applications in the home network can only be performed at the CPE.¶

Shaping at the CPE, in contrast, scales naturally: each CPE shapes only its own traffic using a single shaper, and the required resources are distributed across all subscribers. In addition, the queue is located at the device that has the most information about its traffic, allowing prioritization, flow queuing, and AQM per subscriber without any additional resources in the network.¶

A.3. Why DHCP and Not Manual Configuration or TR-069?

The provisioned rate is typically unknown to the user, may change over time (e.g., due to service upgrades or fair-use policies), and differs from the physical link speed of the CPE, so manual configuration is error-prone and rarely performed. Auto-configuration protocols such as TR-069 [TR069] are not deployed by all operators, are typically not available for customer-owned CPE, and cannot provision intermediate nodes. DHCP, in contrast, is used by virtually all broadband CPE, is processed by the BNG acting as relay agent or server, and is visible to intermediate access nodes. Rates conveyed via DHCP are refreshed with every lease renewal, and the Rate Option is simple enough to be processed by event-driven scripts in existing DHCP clients (see Section 9).¶

A.4. Why Are Rates Maintained per Protocol?

DHCP configuration is normally valid only for the lifetime of the binding from which it was learned. A single set of rates per link, replaced by whichever Rate Option was received last, could not follow this model: when the DHCPv4 lease expires, the rates may still be valid via DHCPv6, and vice versa. Maintaining one rate set per protocol allows each set to be cleared together with its binding.¶

While the binding persists, an absent Rate Option leaves the rate set unchanged, and rates are removed only explicitly by values of 0. This avoids unintended removal by messages that do not carry configuration options, such as DHCPv6 Reply messages sent in response to Confirm messages, and by servers or intermediate devices that do not include the Rate Option in every message. It also keeps the processing simple, as a receiver only acts on Rate Options that are present.¶

When both rate sets exist, a deterministic choice avoids rates that alternate depending on which message was received last. Selecting the lower rate per direction was considered but rejected: it can combine the upstream rate of one rate set with the downstream rate of the other, resulting in a combination that no server signaled. Preferring one protocol as a whole avoids this. DHCPv6 is preferred because it is available in IPv6-only and dual-stack deployments and is commonly the protocol used over PPPoE sessions (see Section 7.1). As operators configure identical rates for both protocols, the choice only matters during transitions, and receivers can apply a different local policy.¶

A.5. Why Only Layer 3 Rates?

Earlier versions of this document allowed the server to select the layer to which the rates refer and to describe the per-packet accounting of the enforcement point, including overhead, minimum packet size, and cell-based link layers, as well as separate informational rates. This allowed a precise model of any bottleneck, but required every receiver to implement the corresponding calculations, to translate parameters between the link of the receiver and the enforcement point, and to handle the interactions between parameters for each direction.¶

Restricting the signaled rates to Layer 3 removes this complexity. A Layer 3 rate closely matches what users perceive, so a single value serves both traffic management and display; it is independent of the encapsulation, so the same value applies at every node; and receivers that compensate for their locally known link-layer headers apply it exactly for all packet sizes, while receivers that cannot perform header compensation err on the safe side (see Section 3.1 and Section 3.3). The remaining inaccuracy only arises when the enforcement point accounts link-layer overhead or the bottleneck is a physical link with per-packet overhead; it can be avoided by Layer 3 accounting at the enforcement point or absorbed by a margin (see Section 8). Precise modeling of legacy cell-based link layers remains possible through local configuration of the CPE.¶

A.6. Why Not Signal a Per-Packet Overhead?

A minimal alternative to the earlier model would signal a single per-packet overhead value in addition to the two rates, allowing the receiver to model the accounting of the enforcement point. This document does not define such a value, for the following reasons:¶

  • Where the receiver and the enforcement point both account traffic at Layer 3, the signaled rate is enforced consistently for all packet sizes, and no overhead information is needed. The link-layer headers of the receiver's own interface are known locally and are handled by header compensation (see Section 3.3).¶

  • A per-packet overhead is only relevant where the enforcement point accounts link-layer overhead or the bottleneck is a physical link. The relevant overhead is then that of the enforcement point, which differs from the encapsulation on the link of the receiver, for example due to VLAN tags added by an access node, and it would require a precise definition of its reference point, such as whether the Ethernet FCS, preamble, and inter-packet gap are included. Encoding overhead that is not per packet, such as the 64/65-octet encapsulation of PTM, or cell-based link layers would require further parameters, such as a minimum packet size or cell size.¶

  • Operators can avoid the need for such information by configuring Layer 3 accounting at the enforcement point (see Section 8), and receivers that require precise modeling of a specific bottleneck can still be configured locally.¶

A.7. Why a Single Fixed-Format Option?

The Rate Option carries exactly two fixed-size values. Compared with a container option with sub-options, this avoids sub-option parsing, a dedicated sub-option registry, and the corner cases of empty or partially valid containers. Compared with separate options per direction, it consumes only one code from the limited DHCPv4 option code space, and a single option conveys the complete rate information, so that receivers never need to combine rates from different options or messages. Receivers that only apply the upstream rate simply ignore the Downstream Rate field. Should additional parameters, such as a per-packet overhead, be required in the future, they can be defined as separate options.¶

A.8. Relation to Access Line Information

Standardized mechanisms exist to convey the characteristics of an access line from the access node to the network: access loop characteristics are carried in DHCP relay agent options and PPPoE tags [TR101], mapped to RADIUS attributes such as Actual-Data-Rate-Upstream and Actual-Data-Rate-Downstream [RFC4679], and reported via ANCP [RFC6320]. These mechanisms inform the BNG and the AAA infrastructure about what the access line can carry. No standardized mechanism, however, conveys the resulting rates to the CPE and to intermediate nodes. This document closes this gap, using the access line information as input for the signaled rates (see Section 8).¶

A.9. Relation to Existing Proprietary Solutions

Several operators have successfully deployed proprietary mechanisms to provide the CPE with its provisioned rate, for example by embedding rate information in PPP authentication messages or by vendor-specific management interfaces. The deployment of such mechanisms demonstrates the need for this information at the CPE, and CPE implementations already provide the corresponding traffic management functions. However, these mechanisms have significant limitations: they are typically supported only by operator-provided CPE, so that customer-owned CPE fall back to the policing behavior described above; rate changes often take effect only after the session is re-established; and the information is not available to intermediate nodes. This document defines a standardized and interoperable alternative that addresses these limitations.¶
